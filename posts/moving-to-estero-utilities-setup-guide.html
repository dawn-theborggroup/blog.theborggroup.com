Moving to Estero, FL: the 2026 complete utilities setup guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 21, 2026·12 min read

How do you set up utilities when you move to Estero, FL?

In Estero, your utility costs reach you three different ways, and that’s what surprises most people moving from up north. Electricity is a monthly FPL bill you open in your own name. Water and sewer come from one of two providers, depending on which side of Williams Road the home sits on. And trash isn’t a bill at all: it’s a prorated charge on your closing statement, and after that a line on your annual property tax bill. Internet may already be built into your HOA dues.

When buyers ask us about moving to Estero, the utilities question usually shows up late — a week before closing, from a hotel room, with movers booked. It’s much easier to sort out before you write an offer, because two of these four costs are decided by the address, not by you. This guide walks through each one the way you’ll actually run into it.

Why Southwest Florida, and why Estero?

The big reasons people choose Southwest Florida still hold: Florida has no state personal income tax, winters are mild, and the Gulf beaches are a short drive away. Estero’s particular draw is position. It sits between Fort Myers and Naples, close to the airport, FGCU, and two hospital systems, with a mix of gated golf communities, resort-style new construction, and established villa neighborhoods. Our complete Estero relocation guide covers the lifestyle side, and our Estero commute guide covers the roads. This one covers the part that turns a house into a working home.

Which utility companies serve Estero?

Here’s the short version, based on the providers the Village of Estero lists for residents:

  • Electric: Florida Power & Light (FPL)
  • Water: Lee County Utilities north of Williams Road; Bonita Springs Utilities south of Williams Road
  • Garbage, recycling, and yard waste: Lee County Solid Waste
  • Internet, TV, and phone: Comcast, Frontier, CenturyLink, and Summit Broadband are the primary providers the Village lists

Natural gas isn’t on the Village’s list. Some homes have it, and many Estero homes are all-electric or use a propane tank for a range, pool heater, or generator. That’s a question to settle for the specific house, which we come back to below.

Electric: FPL and the deposit question

FPL serves Estero, and you’ll start service in your own name for your closing or move-in date. The part relocating buyers don’t expect is the deposit. FPL uses a credit-scoring system to decide whether a new account needs one. If it does, the deposit equals about two months of expected electric use once the home is occupied — FPL’s own example is a $200 deposit on a $100 average monthly bill. FPL refunds the deposit with interest when you close the account, or after 23 months of consecutive service with a clean payment record.

As for the bill itself, the Florida Public Service Commission approved a four-year FPL rate agreement in November 2025. WGCU reported that a typical 1,000-kWh residential bill went from $134.14 to $136.64 starting January 1, 2026. Your usage is the bigger variable. Air conditioning runs most of the year here, and a pool pump adds to it. For a realistic number, ask for the home’s past 12 months of electric bills. A seller usually has them, and they tell you far more than any average.

Water and sewer: the Williams Road line

Estero doesn’t run its own water system. The Village’s own 2019 water supply plan says Lee County Utilities serves the part of the Village north of Williams Road, and the Village’s resident page says Bonita Springs Utilities serves properties south of it. That one road decides who you’ll pay, how you sign up, and what your deposit looks like.

North of Williams Road — Lee County Utilities (LCU). LCU is a county utility. A new single-family account may require a deposit of $70 for water and $125 for sewer. LCU will waive the single-family deposit if you meet one of three conditions: a prior LCU account in good standing within the last two years, a letter from your previous gas, water, or electric provider showing good payment history, or enrolling in both autopay and paperless billing within 20 calendar days of opening the account. That third option is the easy one for most relocating buyers.

South of Williams Road — Bonita Springs Utilities (BSU). BSU is different from anything most people have used up north. It’s a not-for-profit water and wastewater cooperative founded by local residents in 1970, and it’s member-owned. So starting service means becoming a member. BSU asks for identification, a member service application, a W-9 form, and proof of ownership or a lease with the transfer date, plus an optional automatic payment form. None of it is hard, but a W-9 for a water account catches people off guard, and it’s easier to fill out at a desk than in a moving truck.

If your home’s “Estero” mailing address is actually in unincorporated Lee County — which is true of some communities east of I-75 — the same two utilities serve the area, so check the provider by address. Our guide to checking whether a home is inside the Village of Estero explains why the mailing address alone doesn’t settle it.

A few homes aren’t on public water or sewer at all. The Village’s water plan notes some households use permitted private wells, and some older or rural parcels use septic systems. If the home you love is one of them, that changes your inspection plan, so you want to know early.

Irrigation: reclaimed water and watering days

Lawn irrigation in many Estero communities doesn’t run off your household water line. Both LCU and BSU supply reclaimed (reuse) water, and BSU sells its reuse water to Resource Conservation Systems, which supplies irrigation water to Estero communities. In a lot of HOAs, irrigation is handled by the association and folded into your dues. Ask how the home’s sprinklers are fed and who pays for them.

Watering days are set by address and can tighten in the dry season. In 2026, for example, unincorporated Lee County moved to a one-day-a-week schedule from February 1 through the end of May. Rules differ between the Village and unincorporated Lee County, so check the South Florida Water Management District’s watering-days lookup for your address once you move in.

Garbage and recycling: no account to open

This is the one that confuses people the most. Lee County Solid Waste handles garbage, recycling, and yard waste collection in Estero, and you don’t open a monthly account for it. When you buy an existing home, a prorated garbage assessment shows up as part of your closing costs. After that, the charge is billed once a year on your property tax bill. If you buy new construction, the prorated assessment is paid with the certificate of occupancy instead.

Lee County’s posted residential rates for the service areas that include Estero currently run between $312.03 and $327.20 per single-family home per year. Those rates take effect each October 1, and the county directs owners to the Property Appraiser’s site for a specific parcel’s figure. If you’re buying a condo, ask the association how trash is handled, since multifamily buildings often work differently. Because the charge rides on the tax bill, it also gets split at closing like other property tax items — our post on who pays the 2026 property tax bill on a December closing explains how that proration works.

Internet and TV: check the HOA first

Before you call a cable company, ask whether the community already has a bulk agreement. Many Southwest Florida HOAs and condo associations contract internet and TV for every home and include it in the dues — providers like Hotwire specialize in exactly these bulk community contracts. If yours does, you may only need to activate service, not shop for it. If it doesn’t, the Village lists Comcast, Frontier, CenturyLink, and Summit Broadband as primary providers. Availability can vary street by street, so confirm what actually reaches the address before you plan to work from home on day one.

What utilities add to the cost of living in Estero

When you build a monthly budget for an Estero home, utilities sit alongside the bigger fixed costs:

  • Property taxes, which reset at purchase and then benefit from the homestead exemption and Save Our Homes cap if it’s your primary residence
  • Homeowners insurance, and flood insurance where it applies — see our homeowners insurance guide and how to check flood risk in Estero
  • HOA dues and any CDD assessment, which may already cover irrigation, internet, or a guard gate — our guide to reading a CDD budget shows where those line items live
  • Electric and water, your two true monthly utility bills
  • Garbage, which lands on the tax bill once a year rather than monthly

That last point matters when you compare homes. Two houses with the same HOA fee can have very different real costs if one association covers internet and irrigation and the other doesn’t. Our breakdown of the true cost of living in Bonita Springs and Estero puts the whole picture together.

Schools, healthcare, and everyday amenities

Estero sits within reach of two major hospital campuses, Lee Health and NCH, plus urgent care and medical offices along US 41 and Coconut Point. Shopping and dining center on Coconut Point and Miromar Outlets, and FGCU is at the village’s north end. Public schools are part of the School District of Lee County, which assigns students through its own enrollment process; our Estero school enrollment guide for families walks through it. Families should add a school-enrollment check to the same pre-offer list as the utility check, since both depend on the specific address.

What to expect as an out-of-state buyer

Most of our relocating buyers close remotely or arrive just before closing, so utility setup happens from a distance. A few timing notes help:

  • Start electric and water in your name effective the closing date, so there’s no gap and no shutoff. The seller stops their service the same day — BSU, for example, asks departing members for five business days’ notice.
  • Have your documents ready: identification, your contract or closing statement as proof of ownership, and, for BSU, the member application and W-9.
  • Keep a recent letter of good payment history from your current utility if you’re moving into LCU territory and don’t plan to use autopay.
  • Florida gives new residents 30 days to get a Florida driver license and 10 days to title and register a vehicle, according to the Lee County Tax Collector. Utility bills in your name are a common proof-of-address document for both, which is one more reason to set them up early.

If you plan to make Estero your primary home, those same records support your homestead application. Our guide to establishing Florida residency covers the rest of that list.

Common relocation mistakes we see with Estero utilities

  • Assuming the water company from the ZIP code. In Estero, it comes from Williams Road, not the ZIP code or the mailing city.
  • Budgeting for a trash bill that never comes, then being surprised by the prorated garbage line at closing and the assessment on the tax bill.
  • Signing up for internet the HOA already provides, and paying twice for the first few months.
  • Budgeting electric from a national average instead of the home’s own 12-month history, especially with a pool.
  • Learning about a well, septic system, or propane tank at the inspection instead of before the offer.

What would we look at first if you were moving to Estero?

Before you tour, we’d look at which side of Williams Road each home sits on, and whether it’s inside the Village or in unincorporated Lee County. That tells you which water provider you’ll deal with and how you’ll sign up, and it keeps the surprises out of your closing week.

Next, we’d look at what the association actually covers. In Estero, an HOA fee that looks high can be a better value once you see that internet, irrigation, or a staffed gate is already inside it, and one that looks low can leave you paying for all three separately. We’d also flag anything that changes the inspection: a private well, a septic system, a propane tank, or an older pool pump that will drive the electric bill.

Done well, the outcome is simple. You land in Estero with lights on, water running, internet working, and a monthly budget that matches reality, and you compared homes on their true cost rather than their sticker. That’s the part we take care of for you.

Frequently asked questions

Who provides electricity in Estero, FL?

Florida Power & Light (FPL) provides electric service to Estero. FPL may require a deposit of about two months of expected use based on a credit score, refunded with interest after 23 months of good payment history or when you close the account.

Who provides water in Estero, FL?

It depends on location. Lee County Utilities serves the part of the Village of Estero north of Williams Road, and Bonita Springs Utilities serves properties south of Williams Road. Check the provider by address before you buy.

How do I set up trash pickup in Estero?

You don’t open a monthly account. Lee County Solid Waste handles collection, a prorated garbage assessment is part of your closing costs on an existing home, and after that the charge is billed annually on your property tax bill.

Is Bonita Springs Utilities a private company?

Bonita Springs Utilities is a member-owned, not-for-profit water and wastewater cooperative founded in 1970. New customers complete a member service application and a W-9 form along with identification and proof of ownership or a lease.

Can I avoid the Lee County Utilities deposit?

For single-family homes, yes, in three ways: a prior LCU account in good standing within two years, a good-payment letter from a previous gas, water, or electric provider, or enrolling in autopay and paperless billing within 20 calendar days of opening the account.
