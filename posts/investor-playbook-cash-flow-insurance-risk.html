Investor playbook: how to evaluate cash flow and insurance risk on rental properties in Cape Coral, Fort Myers, and Naples

By Mark & Dawn Borg·June 2, 2026·9 min read

Thinking about buying a rental property in Southwest Florida? Before you run the numbers, make sure you're accounting for the costs that can make or break your investment — especially insurance, taxes, vacancy, and realistic rental income.

Southwest Florida continues to attract real estate investors looking for warm weather, strong rental demand, and long-term appreciation. Markets like Cape Coral, Fort Myers, and Naples each offer unique opportunities, but the investment math has changed significantly over the past few years.

In 2026, investors can't rely on old assumptions. Insurance premiums, property taxes, HOA fees, flood zones, and maintenance costs all need to be carefully reviewed before making an offer. A property that looks profitable at first glance may not produce the cash flow you expect once the full cost of ownership is factored in.

Why insurance matters before you calculate cash flow

One of the biggest mistakes rental investors can make in Southwest Florida is underestimating insurance. In areas of Lee County and Collier County, insurance costs can vary dramatically from one property to the next. A canal-front home in Cape Coral may carry a much different insurance profile than an inland property in Fort Myers or a newer home in Naples.

Important factors include: flood zone designation, wind mitigation features, roof age and type, impact windows or storm protection, year built, proximity to water, and prior insurance claims on the property. Before moving forward, investors should get a real insurance quote, not a rough estimate. The insurance number should be part of the investment analysis from the beginning.

What to include in a Southwest Florida rental property pro forma

A strong rental analysis should go far beyond projected rent. When evaluating an investment property in Cape Coral, Fort Myers, Estero, Bonita Springs, or Naples, make sure your numbers include: gross monthly rent, vacancy allowance, property management fees, homeowners insurance, flood insurance (if applicable), property taxes after reassessment, HOA or condo fees, lawn care/pest control/pool service, maintenance reserves, capital expense reserves, and mortgage payment (if financing).

A common mistake is using the seller's current property tax bill. In Florida, a sale can trigger reassessment, meaning the buyer's future tax bill may be much higher than what the current owner is paying.

A non-waterfront Cape Coral home may pencil very differently from a Gulf-access canal home, even if the rental income looks similar.

Market by market: Cape Coral, Fort Myers, and Naples

In Cape Coral, investors need to pay close attention to flood zones, canal-front exposure, and insurance costs — a non-waterfront home may pencil very differently from a Gulf-access canal home, even if the rental income looks similar.

Fort Myers offers a wide range of rental opportunities, from established neighborhoods near McGregor and Iona to newer communities near Gateway and I-75. The tenant base can include workforce renters, healthcare workers, seasonal residents, and families looking for access to schools, employment, and amenities.

Naples generally has higher property values and a stronger luxury tenant pool. However, higher prices can compress cap rates, meaning many Naples investment properties are more appreciation-driven than pure cash-flow plays. For some investors, Naples may still make sense, but the numbers need to be evaluated with a longer-term strategy in mind.

Stress-test the insurance number

A rental property should not only work under ideal conditions. Investors should ask: what happens if insurance increases by 20% next year? What happens if the property sits vacant longer than expected? What happens if maintenance costs are higher than projected? A smart investment analysis includes a base case, a conservative case, and a stress-test case. If the deal only works when everything goes perfectly, it may be too fragile.

Cap rate and cash-on-cash return: know the difference

Two important investor metrics are cap rate and cash-on-cash return. Cap rate measures the return of the property before financing — it helps compare properties across different markets. Cash-on-cash return measures the return on the actual cash invested after debt service, which is especially important when mortgage rates are higher. In today's market, a property may look reasonable on a cap-rate basis but produce weak cash-on-cash returns once financing, insurance, taxes, and reserves are included.

Is Southwest Florida still a good rental market?

Yes, but selectivity matters. Southwest Florida still offers strong long-term fundamentals, including population growth, lifestyle appeal, seasonal demand, and limited coastal land. However, investors need to be disciplined. The best opportunities are often found by investors who know the local market, verify insurance early, understand flood zone risk, use realistic rental projections, negotiate based on actual numbers, and avoid relying on overly optimistic pro formas.

Final thought

Buying an investment property in Cape Coral, Fort Myers, or Naples can still be a smart move, but today's market rewards preparation. The right rental property should be evaluated with real numbers, local knowledge, and a clear understanding of insurance and ownership costs.
