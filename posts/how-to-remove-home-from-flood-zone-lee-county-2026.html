How to get a home removed from a flood zone in Lee County, FL: 2026 buyer's guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 1, 2026·8 min read

Can you get a Southwest Florida home taken out of a flood zone?

Sometimes, and it costs nothing to ask FEMA. If the lowest ground touching the structure sits at or above the Base Flood Elevation, you can request a Letter of Map Amendment, and FEMA's own guidance says a LOMA request is free of charge. What has changed is what the letter buys you. FEMA has discontinued the Preferred Risk Policy that used to be the payoff, so in 2026 the reliable value of a LOMA is not a cheaper premium — it is removing your lender's federal obligation to make you carry the policy at all. And in unincorporated Lee County there is a clock on it: FEMA's Letter of Final Determination dated September 2, 2026 sets the revised Flood Insurance Rate Map as effective March 2, 2027, which can also cancel a flood zone letter a seller already has.

By Mark & Dawn Borg | November 1, 2026

The appeal window on those new Lee County maps is gone — FEMA's determination letter says the statutory 90-day appeal period has elapsed. If you read our earlier rundown of the Lee County FEMA flood map changes, that was the deadline it pointed at.

Here is the part almost nobody tells Southwest Florida buyers: the appeal was never the only route out of a flood zone, and the route that remains has no deadline at all. An appeal challenges the map while it is still being drawn. A LOMA challenges how the finished map treats one specific structure, and you can file it any time. That distinction is worth real money in Bonita Springs, Estero, and Naples, where two homes on the same street routinely sit in different zones and buyers inherit whatever the last owner never corrected.

What to know before you file

1. Confirm which map and which community actually govern the parcel. A Bonita Springs mailing address does not mean the City of Bonita Springs is your floodplain authority, and plenty of homes marketed as Estero sit in unincorporated Lee County. The governing community sets the map, the discount, and the office you call. We walk through that verification in our guide to checking whether a home is inside the Village of Estero.

2. Find out whether an elevation certificate already exists before you pay for one. Under Florida Statute 472.0366, surveyors and mappers have had to send a copy of every elevation certificate they complete to the Division of Emergency Management, within 30 days, since January 1, 2017. The state's public search sits at florida.withforerunner.com/properties. Lee County hosts its own search for unincorporated properties, and the Village of Estero publishes an archive — our Estero flood risk guide covers that lookup step by step. Lee County is candid that nothing may be on file, often because the structure predates the county's first flood maps in September 1984 or sat inside a city limit when it was permitted.

3. Understand the test, because it is not the one most owners assume. A LOMA turns on the Lowest Adjacent Grade — the lowest ground touching the structure — being at or above the Base Flood Elevation. Not the finished floor. A home with a handsomely raised floor in a low-sitting yard does not qualify. And if the structure or lot was raised with fill, the request becomes a LOMR-F instead, which needs your community to sign a Community Acknowledgment Form before FEMA will look at it.

4. Know what FEMA needs in the file. Per FEMA's Online LOMC guidance: an elevation form or existing elevation certificate completed by a licensed surveyor or registered engineer, a FIRM panel or FIRMette, and a tax assessor's map paired with either the property deed or the subdivision plat. Collier County adds the building permit issue date, supplied by its Floodplain Management Section at (239) 252-2942 — and warns that the mailing address printed on the MT-EZ form itself is wrong. Paper filings go to the LOMC Clearinghouse, 3601 Eisenhower Avenue, Suite 500, Alexandria, VA 22304.

5. Plan the timeline on the longer figure. FEMA's own documents do not agree here, which is worth knowing rather than guessing at. The Online LOMC help says amendment requests are reviewed within 60 days of receipt of all required data; the same help text elsewhere says a determination may take up to 90 days. Collier County tells homeowners FEMA will notify the applicant in writing within 60 days. A licensed surveyor filing through FEMA's eLOMA system may get a determination within minutes. Same question, four answers — so if a surveyor is already measuring your lot, the cheapest version of this process is asking whether they can file it electronically while they are there.

The Florida-specific wrinkles

eLOMA will not touch coastal V zones. FEMA's eLOMA guidance excludes requests in coastal Zone V, on alluvial fans, or modified by fill. For much of Bonita Beach and the barrier-island inventory, the fast electronic route is unavailable and the standard filing is the only option.

The premium math is not what it was. Under Risk Rating 2.0, FEMA rates on property-specific variables — flood types, distance from a flooding source, frequency of flood, elevation, and cost to rebuild — and says it can now differentiate property-specific flood risk in non-Special Flood Hazard Areas. FEMA has also said it will no longer offer the Preferred Risk Policy. Being outside the SFHA no longer automatically unlocks a cheap product, and FEMA's own help text advises owners to consider coverage regardless of zone. The dependable win is the requirement going away, not the bill.

Your lender still gets a vote. The FDIC's Consumer Compliance Examination Manual is direct: a lender is not obligated to require mandatory flood insurance for a property no longer in an SFHA, but "the lender has the discretion to continue to require flood insurance if the lender determines it is prudent to do so." The same manual notes lenders are bound by what the FEMA maps show until FEMA changes them, and have no duty to monitor for changes in between. Nobody is watching the map on your behalf.

Your community discount is separate. Lee County reports that owners in unincorporated Lee automatically receive a 25% Community Rating System discount, and Collier County's March 2026 flood protection newsletter puts Collier at Class 5 with a 25% discount on eligible NFIP policies. Those ride on the community's rating, not your individual zone.

Red flags worth slowing down for

  • A seller hands you an old LOMA letter. This is the big one right now. When a revised FIRM takes effect, FEMA publishes a Summary of Map Actions listing every previously issued Letter of Map Change and whether it is incorporated, revalidated, or superseded. For a superseded letter, the original determination is no longer valid and the property is treated as being in the SFHA on the revised map. The revalidation letter takes effect one day after the new map publishes. With unincorporated Lee County's revised map effective March 2, 2027, a letter dated before that carries a question mark.
  • "It's not in a flood zone" offered as a fact rather than a document. Ask which map, which panel, which date, and whether there is a letter.
  • A certificate that is not a finished construction certificate. A construction-drawings or building-under-construction version is not what FEMA wants in a LOMA file.
  • Anyone promising a premium outcome. After Risk Rating 2.0, the only honest number is the one your carrier quotes on your address.
  • Stale government pages. Lee County's own project page for the proposed revisions still shows an earlier timeline than FEMA's September 2, 2026 determination letter. When two official sources disagree, the later document wins — and it is worth a call either way. Lee County Floodplain Management takes those at 239-533-8948.

How a buyer's agent protects you here

Most of this is paperwork nobody is assigned to do. The seller has no reason to file a LOMA on a house they are leaving. The lender is bound by the map in front of it. FEMA will not come find you. The gap closes only when someone on the buyer's side is looking for it.

What would we look at first if you called us about a flood zone letter in Lee County?

The date on it, before anything else. A flood zone letter is tied to one specific map, and unincorporated Lee County gets a new one on March 2, 2027 — so the first question is not whether the letter is real, it is whether it survives the map change. From there we want to know which community actually governs the parcel, because that decides which office, which archive, and which discount apply, and the mailing address gets it wrong often enough that we check every time.

Then we look at whether the underlying elevation work already exists somewhere public, since that is usually the only part of this with a real price tag attached — and at whether the structure even clears the Lowest Adjacent Grade test before anyone spends money finding out. If it plainly does not, we would tell you early and put the inspection budget somewhere it does more good.

What that is worth to you is a straight answer before you waive a contingency instead of a surprise at the closing table, and if the letter does need refiling, knowing it while you still have leverage in the transaction. That's the part we take care of for you.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a FEMA Letter of Map Amendment cost in Florida?

FEMA charges nothing. FEMA's Online LOMC help states that a LOMA request is free of charge, and its eLOMA guidance says there is no cost to submit one or receive a determination. Collier County's homeowner reference repeats that FEMA does not charge a fee to process a LOMA application. What you pay is the licensed surveyor's fee for the elevation certificate, quoted per property.

How long does a LOMA take?

FEMA's own documents give more than one figure, so plan on the longer one. The Online LOMC help says amendment requests are reviewed within 60 days of receipt of all required data, while the same help text elsewhere says a determination may take up to 90 days. A licensed surveyor filing through eLOMA may receive a determination within minutes, which is why the order of operations matters.

Does a LOMA mean my lender has to drop the flood insurance requirement?

It removes the federal mandate, not the lender's judgment. The FDIC's examination manual says a lender is not obligated to require mandatory flood insurance once a property is no longer in a Special Flood Hazard Area, but keeps the discretion to require it anyway if it determines that is prudent. Ask your lender in writing before you count on the savings.

Can a flood zone letter stop being valid?

Yes. When a revised map takes effect, FEMA publishes a Summary of Map Actions listing previously issued Letters of Map Change and whether each is incorporated, revalidated, or superseded. A letter that is not revalidated is no longer valid, and the property is treated as lying in the Special Flood Hazard Area on the revised map. With unincorporated Lee County's new map effective March 2, 2027, any older letter there is worth re-checking.

My house is elevated. Why was the LOMA denied?

A LOMA is decided on the Lowest Adjacent Grade, not the finished floor. A raised floor in low surrounding ground will not qualify. If the structure or lot was raised using fill, the request has to be a LOMR-F, which requires the community to sign a Community Acknowledgment Form first.

A note on sourcing, because stale numbers travel fast on this topic. Every figure above comes from FEMA's own guidance, FEMA's September 2, 2026 Letter of Final Determination for Lee County, Lee County's and Collier County's floodplain pages, the FDIC's examination manual, or Florida Statute 472.0366. We have deliberately not published a dollar figure for an elevation certificate — we could not find a current, dated, verifiable one for Lee or Collier County, and that number should come from a surveyor quoting your address.
