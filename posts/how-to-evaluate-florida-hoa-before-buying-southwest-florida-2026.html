How to evaluate a Florida HOA before buying: a 2026 Southwest Florida buyer's guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·September 20, 2026·8 min read

If you're buying a home in Southwest Florida, you're almost certainly buying into an HOA. To evaluate it properly, request the FS 720.401 disclosure summary, review CC&Rs for rental restrictions, examine the reserve fund (70%+ funded ratio is healthy; below 30% is a red flag), read 12–24 months of board minutes, order an estoppel certificate (capped at $299), and check for pending litigation. Here's exactly how we walk our buyers through each step.

Nearly every gated community in Bonita Springs, Estero, Naples, and Fort Myers operates under a homeowners association. Many have two: a master HOA that governs the overall development and a sub-HOA that manages your specific neighborhood or building.

What buyers don't always realize until after closing is how much financial exposure those HOAs carry. A poorly funded reserve account means the association can't pay for roof replacements, repaving, or pool renovations without levying a special assessment on owners. That assessment becomes your bill — sometimes $5,000, sometimes $30,000, sometimes more — and it lands after you've already purchased.

This is one of the most consistent questions we hear from buyers relocating from out of state: "How do I know if the HOA is in good shape?" Here's exactly how we walk our clients through it.

A step-by-step HOA evaluation checklist for Southwest Florida buyers

Step 1: Request the Florida HOA Disclosure Summary. Under Florida Statute 720.401, the seller is required to give you an HOA disclosure summary within 3 business days of a ratified contract. Once you receive it, you have 3 business days to cancel the contract and receive a full refund of your deposit — no questions asked.

This is your first layer of protection. The disclosure identifies the association, discloses mandatory membership fees and dues, and confirms whether there are any pending special assessments the seller is aware of. Don't let this document sit unread.

Step 2: Review the CC&Rs, Bylaws, and Rules & Regulations. The governing documents tell you what the HOA actually controls. For out-of-state buyers especially, this is where surprises live:

  • Rental restrictions. Some communities prohibit rentals for the first year or two of ownership. Others cap the number of rentals allowed community-wide. If you're buying as an investment or planning to rent while you transition, confirm rental policy before you remove inspection contingencies.
  • Short-term rental prohibitions. The vast majority of gated communities in Southwest Florida explicitly prohibit short-term rentals (30 days or fewer). Bonita Bay, Pelican Landing, The Brooks — virtually all of them. Verify this if it's relevant to your plans.
  • Pet, vehicle, and architectural rules. Many HOAs restrict commercial vehicles, RVs, or boat trailers. Paint colors, landscaping, and exterior modifications typically require board approval. These aren't dealbreakers, but you should know them before closing.

Step 3: Examine the Reserve Fund. This is the single most important financial metric in your HOA evaluation. HOAs are required to maintain reserve accounts for major capital expenses — roof replacements, exterior painting, repaving, pool equipment, elevators. The funded ratio measures how well-funded those reserves are:

  • 70% or higher: Healthy. The association has adequate reserves to cover expected expenses without levying special assessments.
  • 50–70%: Adequate. Watch for planned fee increases or deferred projects.
  • 30–50%: Concerning. Ask specifically what the board's plan is to address the shortfall.
  • Below 30%: Red flag. An underfunded reserve at this level is a meaningful risk of a special assessment. Budget accordingly or factor it into your offer price.

You're looking for the reserve study, not just the current balance. The study will show projected reserves needed over the next 20–30 years and how the current funding compares to that projection.

Step 4: Read the last 12–24 months of board meeting minutes. Minutes are where the real story lives. The formal financial documents tell you what the numbers are. The minutes tell you what the board has been doing about them — and what they've been avoiding.

Look for: repeated maintenance complaints, deferred contractor projects, disputes with vendors, discussions about fee increases, and anything described as "tabled until next meeting" across multiple consecutive months. A single tabled item isn't unusual. The same issue tabled three meetings in a row suggests the board either can't agree or doesn't have the budget to act.

We read every set of minutes we request for our clients, and they've saved more than a few buyers from situations that looked clean on paper.

Step 5: Order the HOA Estoppel Certificate. The estoppel certificate is a legal document that certifies the HOA's financial and compliance status at the moment of closing. Florida law requires it to be delivered within 10 business days, and the fee is capped at $299 per association ($149 if there are no violations or outstanding balances).

This document discloses the current monthly fee amount, whether the seller has any delinquencies with the HOA, any outstanding violations on record for the unit, any pending or approved special assessments, and any active litigation the association is a party to. The estoppel protects you from inheriting the seller's HOA problems — without it, you could close and discover the seller owed six months of dues or had an open violation you're now responsible for resolving.

Step 6: Check for pending litigation. An HOA involved in active litigation is an HOA spending money on legal fees. That money comes from reserve funds or from a special assessment. Either way, it affects you. Active litigation can also complicate financing — some lenders won't issue a mortgage on a unit in a development where the HOA is a named party in an active lawsuit. Ask your agent to confirm litigation status before clearing any contingencies.

Florida-specific nuances that catch out-of-state buyers off guard

CDD fees are separate from HOA fees. In many Southwest Florida communities — especially newer master-planned developments — you'll encounter both. CDD (Community Development District) fees are a government assessment that appears on your property tax bill. HOA fees are a private assessment collected by the association. The two are billed separately and operate independently. A $350/month HOA plus a $5,000/year CDD assessment is a very different monthly cost than the HOA number alone suggests. Our complete guide to CDD fees in Southwest Florida breaks down how to find them for any community you're considering.

Right of first refusal. Some associations — particularly in communities with equity club memberships — include a right of first refusal in their governing documents. This gives the HOA or club the option to purchase your unit at the agreed-upon sale price before it transfers to a third party. It rarely affects routine transactions, but it's worth knowing about before you're under contract.

HOA approval timelines. Most communities in Southwest Florida require buyers to submit an application before closing, and some require an in-person interview. Typical approval timelines run 1–4 weeks. Some equity communities have additional steps. Your agent should contact the HOA management company in the first week of the contract period to confirm the process and timeline.

Mandatory club memberships. Communities like Bonita Bay require buyers to purchase an equity membership in the club — a separate transaction from the real estate purchase that can run $70,000–$90,000 or more depending on the membership tier. This is not included in the purchase price and is not an HOA fee. Know what membership obligations come with the property before you make an offer.

Red flags that warrant a harder look: A reserve funded ratio below 30%, an undisclosed pending special assessment, repeated unresolved maintenance complaints in the minutes, a self-managed HOA with no professional management company, a delinquency rate above 10–15% of homeowners, active HOA litigation, or monthly fees that increased 25% or more in the past 24 months. Your specific situation determines how much weight to give any one of these — that's exactly the kind of analysis we walk buyers through before they clear contingencies.

Frequently asked questions

What is an HOA estoppel certificate in Florida?

An HOA estoppel certificate is a legal document that certifies the HOA's financial and compliance status at the time of closing. Florida law caps the fee at $299 per association ($149 if there's no outstanding balance or violations), and it must be delivered within 10 business days. It discloses monthly fees, any delinquencies, pending special assessments, and active violations — protecting the buyer from inheriting the seller's HOA problems.

What is a healthy HOA reserve fund ratio in Florida?

A funded ratio of 70% or higher indicates strong reserves for anticipated capital maintenance and repairs. A ratio between 50% and 70% is adequate but warrants attention to any planned projects or deferred maintenance. Below 30% is a meaningful red flag — it signals the community may be unable to fund major repairs without levying a special assessment, which becomes your expense as the new owner.

Can I back out of a home purchase because of HOA documents in Florida?

Yes. Under Florida Statute 720.401, once you receive the HOA disclosure summary from the seller, you have 3 business days to cancel the contract and receive a full refund of your deposit. This is sometimes called the HOA rescission period. It applies specifically to HOA disclosure packages and is separate from any inspection contingencies in your purchase agreement.

What is the difference between HOA fees and CDD fees in Southwest Florida?

HOA fees are collected by the private homeowners association to maintain community amenities, landscaping, and shared spaces. CDD fees are a government assessment — they appear on your property tax bill and fund public infrastructure like roads, utilities, and parks built by the developer. Many communities in Southwest Florida have both, billed separately. Always request CDD fee details alongside your HOA documents.

How long does it take for an HOA to approve a buyer in Southwest Florida?

Most HOAs in Southwest Florida require buyers to submit an application, with approval timelines typically running 1–4 weeks. Some communities — particularly those with equity club memberships or more rigorous application processes — take longer. Your agent should contact the HOA management company in the first week of the transaction to confirm the timeline and avoid any closing delays.
