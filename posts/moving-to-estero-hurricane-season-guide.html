Moving to Estero, FL: the 2027 complete hurricane season guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 7, 2026·11 min read

What changes when you move to Estero during hurricane season?

Short answer: your move-in date decides which of Florida’s storm rules you meet first. Hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30. During those six months, a tropical storm watch anywhere in Florida can stop insurers from writing new policies, which can stall a financed closing. A separate hurricane deductible applies to your policy, and Lee County gives evacuation orders by storm surge zone, which is not the same thing as your FEMA flood zone. If you’re moving to Estero in 2027, set your closing date, insurance and evacuation plan around the season.

Most relocation guides treat hurricanes as one paragraph about shutters. That’s not where new Estero residents get caught. They get caught on timing: a closing set for the second week of October, a flood policy started too late, or a deductible picked because it had the lowest premium. Here is how the season works for someone buying here, and what we would plan around.

Why Estero, and why plan around the calendar?

People move to Estero for the reasons you’d expect. It sits between Fort Myers and Naples, close to Southwest Florida International Airport, Coconut Point, Florida Gulf Coast University and the beaches of Bonita and Fort Myers Beach. Florida has no state income tax. Winters are mild, which is why so many of our buyers come from the Northeast and Midwest.

The same calendar that makes January here so good also sets the risk. Southwest Florida’s busiest buying season is winter and spring, but plenty of buyers close in summer and fall, often because a job, a school year or the sale of a home up north sets the date. If you’re closing between June 1 and November 30, three things exist that don’t exist in February.

1. A storm watch anywhere in Florida can freeze new insurance

Citizens Property Insurance, the state-backed insurer, suspends binding whenever “a tropical storm or hurricane watch or warning has been issued by the National Weather Service” for any part of Florida. During a suspension, agents can’t bind new coverage or increase existing coverage, “regardless of effective date.”

Here’s why that matters in Estero. On October 7, 2026, Citizens suspended binding statewide because of Tropical Storm Isaias. Isaias made landfall near Destin, in the Panhandle, nearly 400 miles from Estero in a straight line. A buyer closing in Estero that week was still affected by the suspension. Citizens also suspended binding for Tropical Storm Bertha in July 2026 and lifted it on July 22.

Private insurers make their own rules, but many pause new business the same way. When Hurricane Ian approached in September 2022, the Miami Herald reported that some insurers stopped writing new homeowners and flood policies statewide after a tropical storm watch was issued. That mostly affects financed buyers, because your lender won’t close without proof of coverage. Cash buyers can close, but they carry the risk themselves until the policy starts.

The fix is simple, but it has to happen early: get your policy bound before any storm is on the map, not the week of closing. If you wait for a quote until the last week, one watch in the Gulf can push your closing back.

2. Your flood policy may have a 30-day wait

Federal flood insurance through the NFIP normally has a 30-day waiting period before coverage begins. One exception matters most for buyers: the wait doesn’t apply when the policy is bought “in connection with making, increasing, extending or renewing” a mortgage. A FEMA question-and-answer document provided to the Louisiana Bankers Association explains it this way: the policy takes effect at loan closing “provided that the policy is applied for at or before closing.”

That creates a gap that catches cash buyers. If you pay cash and buy a flood policy on your own, the 30-day wait usually applies. Close on September 15, apply for flood coverage the next week, and your coverage may not begin until mid-October, during the busiest part of the season. If you’re buying with cash, apply for flood coverage before closing, not after you get the keys.

Not sure whether an Estero address needs flood insurance at all? Our guide to checking a home’s flood risk in Estero walks through the maps and documents in order.

3. Your hurricane deductible is a percentage, not a flat amount

Florida homeowners policies carry a separate hurricane deductible. Florida’s Department of Financial Services says insurers must offer options of $500, 2 percent, 5 percent or 10 percent of the policy’s dwelling limit, with some exceptions. The math matters. On a home insured for $500,000:

  • 2% = $10,000 out of pocket before the policy pays a hurricane claim
  • 5% = $25,000
  • 10% = $50,000

The hurricane deductible starts when the National Hurricane Center issues a hurricane warning for any part of Florida, and it ends 72 hours after the last hurricane watch or warning in Florida is lifted. It applies once per calendar year to all hurricane losses with the same insurer. Choosing a higher deductible lowers your premium, but it also means keeping that much cash on hand. Pick a number you could actually pay in October, not just the one that makes the quote look best.

Estero neighborhoods: evacuation zones, not just flood zones

Lee County labels hurricane evacuation zones A through E, along with non-evacuation areas. The Village of Estero explains that the zones are listed “in order of increasing elevation” and are based on storm surge modeling by the National Hurricane Center. Lee County calls evacuations by zone, in phases, for surge and wind.

Many new residents assume a FEMA flood zone tells them whether they’ll be asked to evacuate. It doesn’t. Your flood zone affects insurance and lending. Your evacuation zone tells you when Lee County expects you to leave. They are separate maps for separate purposes. Look up both for every address you’re serious about, using the county’s Know Your Zone tool or the LeePrepares app.

Estero’s communities cover a wide range of land, so don’t judge by the community name. Homes near Estero Bay and the Estero River sit on different land from homes along Corkscrew Road, and elevation can change within one community. Use the address, not the neighborhood, for every lookup. A few places to start comparing communities:

  • The Brooks, the large master-planned area that includes Copperleaf, Shadow Wood and Spring Run
  • Verdana Village and The Place at Corkscrew, newer construction along Corkscrew Road
  • Corkscrew Shores and Bella Terra
  • WildBlue and Wildcat Run

What does living in Estero cost once storm costs are included?

The basics are covered in our true cost of living guide for Bonita Springs and Estero. Hurricane season adds a few line items that many relocation budgets leave out:

  • Your hurricane deductible, which works more like a cash reserve than a premium. See the math above.
  • Flood insurance, required by lenders in high-risk flood zones and worth pricing even where it isn’t required.
  • Opening protection, such as impact windows, shutters or panels. A wind mitigation inspection documents these features, and they can lower your windstorm premium. Our four-point and wind mitigation guide explains how that works.
  • HOA and CDD rules for shutters, panel storage and standby generators. Many associations set rules for where generators can go and when shutters can stay up. Read the rules before you buy equipment.
  • Property taxes. If you plan to make Florida your permanent home, check our post on why Amendment 3 makes December 31, 2026 a date to know. Your residency date can affect your homestead exemption for years.

Healthcare, schools and storm services in Estero

For healthcare, the Village of Estero lists Lee Health Coconut Point at 23450 Via Coconut Point, with an emergency department, primary care and rehabilitation. It also lists Gulf Coast Medical Center in Fort Myers and NCH North Naples Hospital as the closest hospitals. For schools, Estero is served by the School District of Lee County. Our Estero school enrollment guide covers how assignment and enrollment work. Always confirm the assigned school for a specific address with the district.

Before your first season, set up these storm services:

  • AlertLee, Lee County’s free emergency alert system, which sends alerts by phone, text or email about storms and evacuations. It replaced CodeRed.
  • The LeePrepares app, which replaced the old LeeAlert app. It can find your evacuation zone by GPS, show shelter status and give you a supply list.
  • Village of Estero resources: the Village’s preparation guide lists sandbags at Estero Fire Rescue, 21500 Three Oaks Parkway, when available, and names the Estero Recreation Center and Hertz Arena as Estero-area shelters. Shelters open based on each storm, so check which ones are open before you go.

What to expect as an out-of-state buyer

If you’re buying from out of state, you probably won’t be here for every step. That can work well, but during hurricane season the steps need to happen in the right order:

  • Get insurance quotes during your inspection period, not after it. The four-point and wind mitigation reports are what insurers use to price the home.
  • Have your policy bound with time to spare, so a storm watch near your closing date doesn’t stop you.
  • Plan for an empty house. If you close in August and move in November, someone needs to secure the home if a storm comes. Line that up before closing.
  • Know your community’s storm rules, including who handles common-area preparation and what owners must do before leaving.

If insurance is the part that worries you most, our post on whether a buyer can actually insure a Southwest Florida home explains the roof-age and Citizens eligibility rules.

What would we look at first if you called us about closing in Estero during hurricane season?

First, the calendar. We compare your target closing date with the season before we write an offer, because a closing in mid-October carries different risks than one in mid-March. If the timing is tight, we’d rather tell you before you’re under contract than the week a storm forms.

Then we check the insurance path on that specific house. That means looking at roof age, opening protection and the flood picture early enough that the quote is in hand while you still have choices. We look at whether you’re financing or paying cash, because that changes when the flood policy needs to start. We also look at the address’s evacuation zone, because a buyer who plans to spend summers up north needs a different plan than one who will be here year-round.

When this is handled well, you close on the date you planned, with coverage in place, a deductible you chose on purpose and a plan for the first storm. Common hurricane-season relocation mistakes

  1. Treating the flood zone as the evacuation zone. They are different maps. Look up both.
  2. Waiting until closing week to bind insurance. A watch anywhere in Florida can stop new policies. In October 2026, a Panhandle storm stopped Citizens statewide.
  3. Buying flood coverage after a cash closing. The 30-day wait usually applies when the policy isn’t tied to a loan.
  4. Choosing a deductible by premium alone. A 5% deductible on a $600,000 dwelling limit is $30,000 in cash.
  5. Leaving the home unprepared. Seasonal owners need someone in place to secure the home and check it after a storm.
  6. Skipping AlertLee and LeePrepares. They take minutes to set up and are the county’s main way to reach residents.

Frequently asked questions

When is hurricane season in Estero, Florida?

The Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30. The Village of Estero says activity peaks from mid-August to mid-October. If you’re closing or moving during those weeks, plan your insurance and closing dates with extra time.

Can a hurricane delay my home closing in Florida?

Yes. Citizens Property Insurance suspends new policies whenever a tropical storm or hurricane watch or warning is issued for any part of Florida, and many private insurers pause new business the same way. If your lender needs proof of insurance and your policy isn’t bound yet, your closing can be delayed until the suspension is lifted. Binding coverage early is the best way to reduce that risk.

Is my evacuation zone the same as my flood zone?

No. Lee County’s evacuation zones, A through E, are based on National Hurricane Center storm surge modeling and decide when you’re asked to leave. FEMA flood zones are used for flood insurance and lending. Look up both for any Estero address.

Does flood insurance start right away when I buy a home?

If you buy NFIP flood insurance as part of a mortgage and apply at or before closing, it can take effect at closing. If you buy it on your own, such as after a cash purchase, a 30-day waiting period usually applies. Apply before closing so you’re not left without coverage.

What hurricane deductible should I choose?

Insurers must offer $500, 2%, 5% or 10% of the dwelling limit, with some exceptions. A higher percentage lowers your premium, but you pay more out of pocket after a hurricane. Choose an amount you could pay from savings, and confirm the details with a licensed insurance agent.

Planning your first Estero hurricane season

Hurricane season doesn’t need to stop your move to Estero. It just means planning a few things in the right order: your closing date, your insurance, your flood policy, your deductible and your evacuation plan. Get those set, and your first season is a lot less stressful.
