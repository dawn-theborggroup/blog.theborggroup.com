Can you transfer your flood insurance to the buyer? Southwest Florida 2026

By Mark & Dawn Borg·October 15, 2026·8 min read

Can a Southwest Florida seller transfer a flood insurance policy to the buyer?

Yes. If your policy is written through the National Flood Insurance Program, you can assign it to your buyer at closing instead of cancelling it — and FEMA's own Risk Rating 2.0 guidance confirms that the discount and the statutory glide-path rate transfer with the property to the new owner. Private flood policies usually cannot be assigned. In a Southwest Florida market where Estero homes are closing at 92.7% of asking price after 69 days and the 30-year fixed mortgage averaged 7.40%, that one piece of paperwork is among the very few levers a seller still controls.

Here's the thing almost nobody tells Southwest Florida sellers: when your buyer's lender runs the numbers, your sale price is only one line on the worksheet. Insurance is another. And right now, insurance is the line that kills deals.

We get asked about commission, staging, and list price constantly. We almost never get asked about the flood policy sitting in the seller's file cabinet — the one that, in many cases, is worth more to the buyer than a new paint job.

The market you're actually selling into

Let's start with the honest picture, because it shapes everything that follows.

As of mid-October 2026, the trailing twelve-month median sold price in Estero was $495,000, down 5.8% year over year, at $256 per square foot. Homes took a median 69 days on market and closed at 92.7% of asking price, with 421 active listings against 134 under contract — a Momentum Score of 42 out of 100, squarely in buyer's-opportunity territory.

Bonita Springs looks similar: a $529,900 median, down 0.6%, $298 per square foot, 75 days on market, and 91.0% of ask. Lee County as a whole is holding up better, at a $379,025 median, down 1.8%, 59 days on market, and 93.2% of ask.

Then there's financing. Freddie Mac's weekly survey put the 30-year fixed at 7.40% for the week of October 8, 2026 — up from 7.28% the week before, and up from 6.30% a year earlier. The 15-year averaged 6.73%.

So your buyer is absorbing a payment roughly a full point more expensive than a buyer last fall, and they're shopping against 421 other Estero listings. That buyer is not stretching. They're underwriting. And carrying cost — taxes, HOA, CDD, insurance — is where the stretch runs out. If you're still weighing whether to list at all, we worked through that question in our look at whether sellers should wait for mortgage rates to drop.

What assignment is actually worth

Flood insurance is the one piece of that carrying cost a seller can hand over at a discount.

FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 guidance is explicit on two points. First, policyholders can transfer their discount to a new owner by assigning the flood insurance policy when the property changes ownership. Second, statutory glide paths transfer with the sale of a property to the new owner — meaning if your premium has been climbing gradually toward its full-risk rate under the 18% annual cap Congress imposed, your buyer steps onto that same glide path rather than starting at today's full rate.

For a home that has carried an NFIP policy for years, that gap can be substantial. A buyer quoted a fresh policy at full-risk rating may pay well above what you are paying for identical coverage on the identical structure. Our post on how flood insurance costs feed into Southwest Florida home pricing goes deeper on why that number moves offers.

Two honest limits. NFIP building coverage caps at $250,000, so this is a floor rather than a complete answer on a higher-value home. And private flood policies are a different story — most private carriers historically have not allowed assignment, though a few now do. Before you promise anything in a listing, find out which kind of policy you have.

There's a local wrinkle worth knowing, too. Your community's FEMA Community Rating System class sets an automatic NFIP discount, and it isn't uniform across our market. Unincorporated Lee County, the City of Bonita Springs, and Collier County all hold a Class 5 rating worth 25% inside the special flood hazard area. The Village of Estero holds Class 6, worth 20% inside the flood zone and 10% outside it. Estero sellers should know that number rather than overselling it — and Bonita and Naples sellers have a slightly better one to mention.

Timing: the two dates that matter

Two calendar items decide whether this works.

Your renewal date. An NFIP policy has to be in force and paid to be assignable. If your renewal falls between contract and closing, pay it — don't let it lapse while you wait to see whether the deal closes. A lapsed policy can't be assigned, and the glide path dies with it.

Your closing date. The assignment has to be coordinated so there is no gap in coverage. Tell your title company and your insurance agent as soon as you are under contract, not the week of closing.

Worth noting on the broader insurance picture: Citizens announced in March 2026 that 2026 homeowners multiperil rates would fall by an average of 8.8% statewide, with wind-only policies down an average of 5.5%, effective July 1 for new policies and at renewal for existing ones. County-level analysis of that filing put Lee County at roughly −4.3% (an average premium moving from about $2,923 to $2,797) and Collier at about −4.1% ($3,470 to $3,328). Those are averages, not your house — but they mean a buyer who priced insurance eighteen months ago is working from a stale number, and it is in your interest to get them a current quote.

The 2026 hurricane season also helped. NOAA's August update called for 7 to 13 named storms and gave a 75% chance of a below-normal season, and Hurricane Isaias — the season's significant Florida landfall, near Destin on October 9 as a Category 2 — came ashore in the Panhandle, hundreds of miles from Lee and Collier County. Our stretch of coast was spared. Buyers don't always know that.

Four mistakes we see sellers make

1. Cancelling the policy at closing instead of assigning it. This is the big one. A pro-rated refund of a few hundred dollars is not worth destroying a transferable rate your buyer would value at multiples of that — and that might have made the difference in getting them to the table.

2. Not pulling the elevation certificate. You may not need to pay a surveyor. The Village of Estero runs a public lookup on Forerunner at esterofl.withforerunner.com that returns a property's flood zone, Base Flood Elevation, and Design Flood Elevation by address, plus a searchable archive of elevation certificates already on file. Collier County will tell you whether it has one for your structure — call the flood information line at 239-252-2942 or email FloodInfoRequest@collier.gov, and check the map at collier.gov/floodmap. An elevation certificate in the listing file removes a week of buyer uncertainty for free. If your zone has changed recently, read our rundown of the Lee County FEMA flood map changes.

3. Getting the flood disclosure wrong. Since October 1, 2024, Florida Statute 689.302 has required residential sellers to disclose, at or before the time the contract is executed, whether they have filed an insurance claim related to flood damage and whether they have received federal assistance for flood damage. This isn't optional and it isn't a negotiating position. Our guide to the Florida Seller's Property Disclosure walks through the rest of the form.

4. Handing over a wind mitigation report on the wrong form. Florida's Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form was revised to OIR-B1-1802 (Rev. 04/26), and any inspection performed on or after April 1, 2026 must use it. The old Rev. 01/12 is still accepted only where the structure is unchanged and the inspection was completed within the five years before that date. A completed form stays valid for five years. If yours is from 2019, it's expired — and an expired report is worse than none, because it looks like diligence and delivers nothing. Our post on Florida four-point and wind mitigation inspections covers what each one tests.

How we market a listing around this

When we take a Southwest Florida listing, we build an insurance packet before the photos go up. That means the current flood declarations page with the policy type identified, the elevation certificate (pulled free where the municipality has one), a wind mitigation report on the current form, a four-point inspection where the home's age calls for it, and the Statute 689.302 disclosure completed properly.

Then we put the assignable-policy language in the listing remarks, because the buyer's agent and the buyer's lender are the two people who most need to see it.

It is unglamorous. It also answers the question that stalls more Southwest Florida deals than any other — what is this actually going to cost me every year? — before a buyer has to ask it. In a market at 92.7% of ask, you want that question answered on your terms.

Your specific numbers depend on your policy type, your flood zone, your municipality's CRS class, and your renewal date. That's a thirty-minute conversation, and it's one we have with every seller before we talk about price.

Frequently asked questions

Can I transfer my homeowners insurance to the buyer too?

No. Homeowners and wind policies are not assignable in Florida — they're underwritten to you, and they end when you sell. Flood is the exception, and only when the policy is written through the NFIP. Your buyer will need their own homeowners policy regardless.

Does my buyer keep my exact premium after assignment?

They keep the discount and the statutory glide path, which is the valuable part. The insurer may still review the new owner and the property, so the figure can shift. Treat your current declarations page as a strong indication rather than a guarantee, and have your buyer confirm with the carrier before they waive contingencies.

What if my home isn't in a flood zone — is any of this relevant?

Yes, in two ways. A property outside the special flood hazard area in Estero still earns a 10% CRS discount on an eligible NFIP policy, and a low-cost policy you've carried voluntarily is still assignable. Just as important, documenting that the home sits in Zone X is itself a selling point in a market where buyers assume the worst. See our breakdown of which Southwest Florida flood zones to buy and avoid.

Will falling insurance rates help me sell?

They help at the margin. We looked at this in detail in our analysis of whether falling insurance is actually helping Southwest Florida sellers — the short version is that carrier rate cuts move slowly and reach buyers only at renewal, while a documented, assignable policy in your listing file reaches them immediately.

How much are my closing costs going to be on top of all this?

Florida doc stamps run $0.70 per $100 of the sale price and are paid by the seller — about $3,465 on a $495,000 Estero sale — before commission, title, and prorations. Our guide to seller closing costs in Lee and Collier County has the full breakdown.
