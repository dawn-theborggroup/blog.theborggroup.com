Copperleaf at The Brooks, Estero FL: why does Estero’s lowest-density bundled golf cost the most in 2026?

By Mark & Dawn Borg·October 23, 2026·11 min read

Copperleaf at The Brooks is a gated, all-resale bundled golf community of 570 homes — 416 single-family houses and 154 carriage homes — wrapped around a single 18-hole championship course in Estero, FL. That works out to roughly 32 homes per golf hole, the lowest ratio of any bundled golf community in Estero. It is also the most expensive one to carry: the club’s own 2025 resident membership sheet totals $14,828 a year, and that figure comes before the Community Development District assessment on your tax bill, before your neighborhood association, and before the beach club, which is sold separately.

Those two facts are the same fact. Spread one golf course across 570 homes instead of 966, and each home carries a bigger share of it. So the question before you tour is not whether Copperleaf is low-density — it is. The question is what that density buys you, and whether the tee sheet is as quiet as the ratio suggests.

What Copperleaf actually is

Copperleaf sits inside The Brooks, the 2,492-acre master-planned development at Coconut Road and Three Oaks Parkway that also holds Spring Run, Lighthouse Bay, Shadow Wood, and Shadow Wood Preserve. Bonita Bay Group was the master developer; Pulte built the homes, concentrated in 2001 and 2002 across seven plat phases.

The course opened in 2000 and turned over to residents in 2002 — an Audubon-certified, par-72 Gordon G. Lewis design, redesigned in 2015 by Kipp Schulties, playing from 4,501 yards at the red tees to 7,066 at the black. Nine sets of tees is unusual for a bundled club and the most practical thing about the place: a 6 handicap and a 26 handicap can play the same round.

The 154 carriage homes sit in two enclaves — Sago Pointe and Wisteria Pointe — at roughly 1,486 to 1,850 square feet with a one-car garage. Single-family homes run roughly 1,730 to 2,875 square feet, and about 88% front the golf course.

Beyond golf: a 27,000-square-foot clubhouse rebuilt in the $20 million Project 2024 renovation, a 3,800-square-foot fitness center, four lighted Har-Tru courts, league bocce, and a 1.7-mile walking loop. Pickleball is not on Copperleaf’s own campus — that is at the shared Brooks courts on Coconut Road, which matters if pickleball is why you are moving.

The density math, and why it cuts both ways

Here is where Copperleaf sits against the other bundled golf communities we write about in Estero:

  • Copperleaf: 570 homes, 18 holes — about 32 homes per hole
  • Breckenridge: 842 homes, 18 holes — about 47 homes per hole
  • Spring Run at The Brooks: 847 homes, 18 holes — about 47 homes per hole
  • Pelican Sound: 1,299 homes, 27 holes — about 48 homes per hole
  • Villages at Country Creek: 966 homes, 18 holes — about 54 homes per hole

Copperleaf is the clear outlier, and the club markets that fact. It is real. But a homes-per-hole ratio only tells you about access if the tee sheet is closed — and Copperleaf’s is not.

The club reported more than 1,300 members as of 2025 and roughly 50,000 rounds a year. It also sells memberships to people who do not live there: up to 55 Associate golf memberships a year, with a $750 non-refundable waitlist deposit, plus up to 20 Social memberships. So the 570 homes are the floor of the tee sheet, not the ceiling.

A homes-per-hole ratio tells you how the cost is divided. It only tells you about tee times if the course is residents-only.

Pelican Sound is residents-only, capped at its 1,299 households. Villages at Country Creek allows outside public play, so you share capacity with anyone who books. Copperleaf sits between the two: a limited outside program rather than an open tee sheet or a closed one.

That is not a knock — those memberships are revenue that holds resident dues down, and the program is published rather than buried. But if you are buying here for easy winter tee times, ask the pro shop what the January tee sheet looks like at 8 a.m., and how many associate slots were filled this year.

What you actually pay in 2026

Copperleaf publishes its resident membership terms, which is more than most bundled clubs in Southwest Florida do. From the club’s 2025 resident membership sheet:

  • Annual dues: $10,500
  • Project 2024 assessment: $3,500
  • Cable and internet: $828
  • Total annual assessment: $14,828
  • Annual food and beverage minimum: $1,000

Read the second line again. The headline dues figure is $10,500, but the assessment funding the clubhouse rebuild adds a third again on top. Anyone who quotes Copperleaf’s dues and stops at $10,500 has quoted you about 71% of the bill. The club’s 2024 sheet listed dues of $11,715 with no separate project line, so a two-year-old number will mislead you in either direction.

At the closing table, the master association charges a one-time capital contribution of $15,000 for a new resident, or $5,000 if you already own here. Add a $250 estoppel fee, and member cart fees of about $31 for 18 holes.

Two more numbers you will have to ask for, because they are not published: carriage home owners at Sago Pointe and Wisteria Pointe pay a neighborhood association fee on top of the master assessment, and some neighborhood associations require their own capital reserve contribution at closing. The HOA estoppel letter for the specific unit is the only reliable place to get them.

The CDD nobody mentions at the sales center

Copperleaf is inside the Brooks of Bonita Springs II Community Development District, established November 30, 1999. A CDD is a unit of local government, not an association, so its assessment lands on your Lee County property tax bill rather than your HOA statement — which is exactly why buyers miss it. Our guide to CDD fees in Bonita Springs and Estero explains why that line deserves as much attention as the association fee, and why the operations-and-maintenance half of it does not expire when the bonds are paid off.

For FY2027 the Brooks districts assess a flat $1,017.25 per residential unit for operations and maintenance, with debt service varying by neighborhood. Published Brooks II neighborhood totals run from about $1,569 to $4,064 per unit per year, depending on which bond series your parcel sits in and how far it has been paid down. We will not guess which end of that range your house is in — the district’s budget is organized by neighborhood, and no public document we found maps every Copperleaf neighborhood to its series. Pull the tax bill for the parcel. The district office at (239) 435-3988 will confirm it, and that figure belongs in your math before you write an offer.

One quirk confuses nearly everyone who looks this up: the district is named for the wrong city. Your mail comes to a Bonita Springs 34135 address and the CDD carries “Bonita Springs” in its legal name — but the district’s own filings place it in southern Lee County within the boundaries of the Village of Estero. The name and the ZIP are fossils of a plat recorded in 1999, fifteen years before Estero incorporated. Your jurisdiction, your millage, and the city you call about a permit are Estero. Comparing cost of ownership across the city line? Start from the jurisdiction, not the mailing address.

Homes, pricing, and what the market is doing

Over the trailing twelve months through July 2026, Copperleaf recorded 29 closed sales at a median of $725,000, ranging from $425,000 to $1,290,350, at a median $388.57 per square foot. Homes sold at a median 96.67% of list after 48 days on market.

Across Lee County, the median home takes 59 days and settles at 93.2% of its original ask. Copperleaf moves faster and holds more of its ask — which says inventory is thin, not that the market is hot: active listings have run from the single digits to the low teens through 2026, around two and a half months of supply.

Practically, that means less negotiating room than elsewhere in Estero, and a real cost to waiting on a home you like — with the 30-year fixed at 7.40% in Freddie Mac’s mid-October survey, indecision is not free either.

Taxes, flood, and the rest of the carrying cost

Your property tax bill resets to your purchase price at closing. Florida’s Save Our Homes cap protects the seller’s assessed value, not yours, and it lifts at sale. On a $725,000 purchase in Lee County, budget $7,200 to $8,700 a year — very likely more than the seller pays today.

On flood: Copperleaf maps predominantly into Zone X, sitting inland east of US-41, with Zone AE and AH pockets along the lake and preserve edges — AH base flood elevation 15 feet, FIRM panel 12071C0593H effective November 17, 2022. The community reported no storm surge in Hurricane Ian. Zone X does not mean no flood insurance; it means it is usually not federally required, and a lender or carrier can still ask. Our walkthrough on checking a home’s flood risk in Estero covers pulling the determination yourself before you are under contract.

One advantage buyers rarely price in: Lee County carries a Class 6 Community Rating System classification, worth a 20% discount on National Flood Insurance Program premiums countywide. And because these homes date to 2001 and 2002, order a wind mitigation inspection early — roof attachment and opening protection on a home of this vintage can move a homeowners premium either way.

The beach club is a separate decision

Copperleaf’s bundled assessment buys the golf course, the clubhouse, and Copperleaf’s own amenities. It does not buy The Commons Club, the shared amenity organization for all five Brooks communities — and the Gulf beach club sits inside The Commons Club, not inside your dues. Its 2025 membership packet lists three tiers, each with a non-refundable equity contribution and annual dues:

  • Bronze — $1,000 equity, about $490 a year. The Rookery restaurant, the Enrichment Center, social activities, and pickleball.
  • Silver — $6,000 equity, about $1,831 a year, plus a $125.98 Health & Lifestyle Center renovation fee payable through 2030. Adds the Health & Lifestyle Center.
  • Gold — $12,000 equity, about $2,385 a year, plus the same renovation fee. Adds the Beach Club — private Gulf beach, chair and umbrella service, boat docks, and kayak and paddleboard rentals.

If the beach is why The Brooks is on your list, Gold is the tier that delivers it: roughly $12,000 up front plus about $2,500 a year — on top of your $14,828, on top of your CDD, on top of your neighborhood fee. Better to see the full stack on one page now than to find layers three and four after closing.

How to buy here without a surprise

Three things decide whether Copperleaf works financially for you, and none is the list price: which bond series your parcel sits in, whether you are buying a carriage home with a neighborhood fee stacked on the master assessment, and whether you will actually use a Gold Commons Club membership or are paying for a beach you visit twice a year.

What would we look at first if you called us about Copperleaf?

The tax bill, before anything else. Not the listing’s fee field — the actual Lee County bill for that parcel, because the Brooks II debt service varies by neighborhood by thousands of dollars a year, and a listing that reports “HOA fee” without the district line is describing maybe two-thirds of what you will owe. Then the estoppel letter and the neighborhood association’s budget, because the carriage enclaves carry a second association that the club’s own material never mentions.

Then something less obvious: the Project 2024 assessment. A $3,500 annual line funding a $20 million clubhouse has a start and an end, and we would want the association’s own documentation of the repayment schedule rather than infer it — because the difference between “this ends in a few years” and “this is permanent” changes your twenty-year number substantially. We have looked at enough Southwest Florida fee structures since 2005 to know the capital line is where the variation hides, not the dues line everyone quotes.

What you should walk away with is one honest annual figure with every layer in it, and a clear answer on what happens to it in five years. That is the part we take care of for you.

How it compares

Against Pelican Sound: Pelican Sound’s club assessment is $11,495 a year for 27 holes, four restaurants, and a Gulf-access boat ramp, with a five-figure payment at closing and residents-only play. Copperleaf is about $3,300 a year more for one course and no boat ramp, but a third of the homes per hole and nine sets of tees. If you boat, Pelican Sound. If you want the quietest course and a rebuilt clubhouse, Copperleaf.

Against Shadow Wood at The Brooks: the same gates, the same beach club, the opposite golf model. At Shadow Wood golf is optional and non-equity across 54 holes, with a waitlist — you can buy there and not pay for golf at all. At Copperleaf it comes with the deed whether you play or not. Shadow Wood if you are unsure you will play; Copperleaf if you know you will, because optional-with-a-waitlist is the worst of both worlds for a committed golfer.

Frequently asked questions

Is Copperleaf in Estero or Bonita Springs?

Copperleaf is in the Village of Estero, in Lee County. The mailing address reads Bonita Springs 34135 and its Community Development District is legally named “Brooks of Bonita Springs II,” both of which predate Estero’s 2014 incorporation. The district’s own filings place it within the boundaries of the Village of Estero, and that is the jurisdiction that governs your millage and your permits.

Is golf membership mandatory at Copperleaf?

Yes. Copperleaf is a bundled golf community, so club membership is tied to ownership. You become a resident member at closing and pay the annual assessment whether or not you play. There is no social-only tier for residents.

What does it cost to own at Copperleaf per year?

The club’s 2025 resident sheet totals $14,828 a year, made up of $10,500 in dues, a $3,500 Project 2024 assessment, and $828 for cable and internet, plus a $1,000 annual food and beverage minimum. On top of that you pay the Brooks II CDD assessment on your tax bill, a neighborhood association fee if you buy a carriage home, and property taxes reset to your purchase price. Verify all of it against the specific parcel.

Does Copperleaf have a CDD?

Yes. Copperleaf sits in the Brooks of Bonita Springs II Community Development District, established in 1999. FY2027 operations and maintenance is a flat $1,017.25 per residential unit, and published Brooks II neighborhood totals range from roughly $1,569 to $4,064 per unit per year depending on bond series and paydown. It appears on your Lee County tax bill, not your HOA statement.

Does the Copperleaf fee include the beach club?

No. The Gulf beach club belongs to The Commons Club, the shared amenity organization for all five Brooks communities, and it is a separate optional membership. The Gold tier is the one that includes the Beach Club, at $12,000 in equity and roughly $2,385 a year plus a renovation fee.

Is Copperleaf in a flood zone?

Copperleaf maps predominantly into Zone X, with Zone AE and AH pockets along the lakes and preserves, on FIRM panel 12071C0593H effective November 17, 2022. Lee County’s Class 6 Community Rating System classification carries a 20% NFIP discount. Pull the flood determination and the elevation certificate for the specific parcel before you make an offer.

The bottom line

Copperleaf earns its reputation. Thirty-two homes per hole, nine sets of tees, a rebuilt clubhouse, and a published fee sheet make it one of the more transparent bundled clubs in Estero — and transparency is rarer than low density around here.

But the advertised number is $10,500 and the real one starts at $14,828, climbs with a CDD assessment that varies by neighborhood by thousands, adds a neighborhood association if you buy a carriage home, and adds about $2,500 a year more if you want the beach. Four layers, three of which nobody volunteers. Get all four on one page before you tour and Copperleaf is a straightforward decision. Skip that step and the surprise arrives with your first tax bill.
