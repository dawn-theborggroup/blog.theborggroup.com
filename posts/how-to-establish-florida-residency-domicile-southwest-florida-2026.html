How to establish Florida residency in Southwest Florida: 2026 domicile guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·September 27, 2026·7 min read

How do you establish Florida residency for tax purposes?

You establish Florida residency by proving both presence and intent: buy or lease a home here, spend the majority of your year in it, and take a specific set of legal steps — Florida driver's license, voter registration, vehicle registration, a filed Declaration of Domicile, and, if you own, a homestead exemption application by March 1. Florida itself sets no minimum day count. The 183-day rule you've heard about belongs to your old state, not this one, and it's the reason half-measures get audited.

Every fall, we get a version of the same call. A couple has been wintering in Bonita Springs or Estero for three, five, sometimes ten years. They finally decide this is the year they make it official — sell the place up north, or keep it and just stop pretending Florida is the "second" home. Then they ask the question that trips up almost everyone: okay, so what do we actually have to do?

The truth is that Florida doesn't require much of anything. There's no form that stamps you a resident, no waiting period, no minimum number of nights. The requirements come from the state you're leaving. New York, New Jersey, Illinois, and other high-tax states don't let go of a taxpayer just because they say so — they make you prove it, and they audit the people who don't.

Why this actually matters (and where people get burned)

If you split time between two states and don't formally establish Florida domicile, your old state can keep taxing you as a full resident — on all your income, not just what you earn there. We've seen retirees discover this the hard way, two or three years after they thought they'd "moved," when a state tax notice shows up referencing the exact number of days they spent in each place.

The 183-day rule is the trigger most people know about: spend six months and a day in your old state during the year, and that state can still claim you as a resident for tax purposes. But the day count is only part of it. Tax auditors in high-tax states look at where your life actually happens — your doctors, your driver's license, where your mail goes, where you're registered to vote. A declaration of domicile filed in Lee County doesn't mean much to a New York auditor if your dentist, your accountant, and your grandkids' school addresses are all still up north.

This is exactly why online communities for snowbirds are full of long threads comparing notes on residency audits. It's a real, specific anxiety for people at this exact decision point, and it's completely avoidable if you do the steps in order and keep the paperwork.

A declaration of domicile doesn't survive an audit when the doctors, club memberships, and billing addresses all stay in the former state. Consistency across every document is what actually holds up.

The step-by-step Florida domicile checklist

Here's the sequence we walk clients through, based on how Florida counties, including Lee and Collier, and the IRS actually process each step:

  1. Close on or move into a Florida home you intend to occupy as your primary residence. This is the anchor. Everything else follows from having a real Florida address and a closing date on record with a title company.
  2. Get your Florida driver's license within 30 days of establishing residency, and surrender your old state's license the same day. A Florida Class E license runs about $48, plus a small service fee at most tax collector offices.
  3. Register your vehicles in Florida within 10 days of establishing residency — cars, trucks, RVs, and boats all count.
  4. Register to vote in Florida and cancel your prior state registration. You can register online if you already have a Florida driver's license and your Social Security number on hand.
  5. File a Declaration of Domicile with the Clerk of the Circuit Court in your Florida county. Lee County and Collier County each have their own version of the form — there's no single statewide template. It's a short, notarized document stating Florida is your permanent home, and it's typically recorded the same day for well under $25.
  6. File for your Homestead Exemption by March 1 of the year following your move, if you own the home. Form DR-501 goes to your county property appraiser and can reduce your home's taxable value by as much as $50,000, on top of the long-term protection of Florida's Save Our Homes cap, which limits how much your assessed value can rise each year once you qualify.
  7. File a final part-year resident return in your old state for the year you moved, and submit IRS Form 8822 to update your address with the federal government.
  8. Move the rest of your life, not just your address. Switch your bank accounts, credit card statements, and retirement distributions to your Florida address. Change doctors, dentists, and your accountant to Florida-based providers where you reasonably can.

None of these steps alone makes you a Florida resident. Together, with dates that line up and a closing that anchors the timeline, they build a case your old state can't easily unwind.

Florida-specific nuances that trip up snowbirds

A few details are easy to miss if you're doing this without local guidance:

  • Doc stamps and title company involvement start the paper trail. Every Florida closing runs through a title company, and the doc stamp tax on your deed, generally $0.70 per $100 of sale price, creates a dated, recorded transaction — one of the clearest pieces of evidence you can hand an auditor. We walk clients through exactly what to expect at closing in Florida before they get there.
  • HOA and condo buyers should read the fine print before day one. If you're buying into a community with HOA approval requirements, an estoppel letter, or CDD fees, those closing costs and timelines are separate from, but happen at the same time as, your residency clock starting.
  • Homestead exemption isn't automatic and it isn't retroactive. Miss the March 1 deadline and you wait a full year for the tax benefit, even though your residency itself already started.
  • Renting out your old home doesn't reset the clock, but it doesn't hurt your Florida case either. What matters most is where your documented life is, not what you did with the old property.

Red flags that invite an audit

High-tax states typically look at your first one to three tax years after the move. These are the mismatches that get flagged most often:

  • You spent 183 days or more back in your old state during the tax year, even if the days weren't consecutive, and even partial days can count.
  • Your doctors, dentists, attorney, and accountant are still based in your old state.
  • You're still claiming a tax break, like a homestead or STAR exemption up north, that's only available to residents of that state.
  • Your kids are still enrolled in schools up north instead of in Florida.
  • Your credit card statements, brokerage accounts, and club memberships still list your old address.

If even a few of these apply to you, a filed Declaration of Domicile won't carry much weight on its own. Consistency across every document is what actually holds up.

How a buyer's agent protects you through this

This is where a lot of snowbirds underestimate what a local agent actually does. Buying the Florida home is step one on this whole checklist, and how that closing is structured, timed, and documented matters more than most buyers realize until it's too late to fix.

We walk our clients through the timing of their closing date relative to their residency goals, connect them with local title companies who handle these closings daily, and make sure the paperwork trail — deed, doc stamps, HOA or CDD disclosures, homestead application timing — lines up cleanly from day one. It's exactly the kind of question we help buyers think through before they ever sign a contract, not after.

Every situation is a little different depending on your old state, your income sources, and how quickly you plan to sell your northern home. The checklist above gets you most of the way there, but the only way to know your specific timeline is right is to talk it through with someone who's guided other Southwest Florida buyers through the exact same decision.
