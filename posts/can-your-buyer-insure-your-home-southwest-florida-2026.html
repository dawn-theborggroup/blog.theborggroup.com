Can your buyer actually insure your home? Southwest Florida 2026

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 5, 2026·8 min read

In Southwest Florida, the buyer's insurance quote is the quietest deal-killer in the 2026 market — and most of what decides it is written down in Florida law before anyone picks up a phone. Section 627.7011(5), Florida Statutes, limits when a carrier may refuse a policy over the age of your roof. Section 627.351(6) sets hard eligibility walls at Citizens, including a $700,000 dwelling replacement cost bar and a separate $750,000 insured-value bar for homes in the wind-borne debris region without opening protection. Lee County's median single-family home went under contract in 58 days in September 2026, then took another 34 days to actually close. That 34-day gap is where the insurance answer lands, and it is the one stretch of your timeline you cannot shorten by pricing better.

Every seller we talk to arrives with a pricing question. Almost none arrive with an insurability question — until a buyer's quote comes back and the conversation is suddenly about a roof instead of a number.

The 34-day window nobody prices for

Start with the timeline. Per Florida Realtors' September 2026 market reports, Lee County single-family homes posted 1,116 closed sales, a median sale price of $375,500, 6,517 active listings (down 13.7%), and 5.2 months of supply. Median time to contract was 58 days; median time to sale, 92.

Subtract one from the other and you get 34 days between a signed contract and a closed sale. Condos and townhouses in the same report ran 86 days to contract and 120 to sale — the same 34. Whatever else separates those two markets, the back half of the transaction takes the same time.

Pricing, photography, and staging all work on the first number. They shorten the 58 days. Nothing you do before listing shortens the 34 — but what you do beforehand decides whether those 34 days stay quiet or turn into a renegotiation. Lee County sellers received a median 94.7% of original list price in September, so buyers are already negotiating. An insurance surprise in week three hands them a reason to do it again, with leverage.

What Florida law actually says about your roof

Here is the part most sellers have backwards. "No carrier will write a roof over 15 years old" gets repeated all over Southwest Florida. The statute is more specific than the rumor, and more favorable to you.

Under s. 627.7011(5)(b), Florida Statutes, an insurer "may not refuse to issue or refuse to renew a homeowner's policy" on a roof that is less than 15 years old "solely because of the age of the roof."

Under s. 627.7011(5)(c), if your roof is 15 years old or more, the insurer "must allow a homeowner to have a roof inspection performed by an authorized inspector" at your expense before requiring replacement as a condition of writing or renewing — and may not refuse solely because of roof age if that inspection "indicates that the roof has 5 years or more of useful life remaining." The subsection applies to policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2022.

An "authorized inspector" under s. 627.7011(5)(a) is a broad category — licensed home inspectors, certified building code inspectors, licensed contractors and roofing contractors, licensed engineers, and licensed architects among them.

Then there is the detail that catches people out. Under s. 627.7011(5)(d), roof age is measured from the last date on which 100 percent of the roof surface was built or replaced under the code in effect at that time. If you re-roofed one slope after a storm and the rest of the surface dates to the original build, the clock did not reset. Your memory of "we did the roof a few years ago" and the underwriter's calculation can be a decade apart.

None of this makes an old roof a non-issue. It means the question has a documented answer and you have a statutory right to an inspection rather than an ultimatum — worth knowing in week one, not week eleven.

Citizens is not the backstop most sellers assume

Sellers often shrug off insurability because Citizens Property Insurance exists: if nobody else writes it, the state will. The statute does not work that way, and Citizens is far smaller than it was.

Citizens' own policies-in-force reporting put it at 216,432 policies as of October 30, 2026. Its September 30, 2026 detail showed 254,836 policies, of which 250,845 were personal lines, down from 769,316 a year earlier — a decline of roughly 67% in twelve months.

More to the point, three provisions of s. 627.351(6) decide whether your home can go there at all:

  • The 20 percent test. For a primary residence, a risk "is not eligible for any policy issued by the corporation unless the premium" from an authorized insurer for comparable coverage "is more than 20 percent greater than the premium for comparable coverage from the corporation." A private offer that is merely expensive does not qualify you — it has to be more than 20% above Citizens. Renewals have used the same test since April 1, 2023. For non-primary residences the same test applies, and the competing offer may come from an authorized insurer or an approved surplus lines insurer.
  • The $700,000 bar. Effective January 1, 2017, a structure with a dwelling replacement cost of $700,000 or more is "not eligible for coverage by the corporation." The statute lifts that ceiling to a replacement cost of less than $1 million in counties where the office finds there is not a reasonable degree of competition. We could not establish which counties currently carry that designation, so treat the $700,000 figure as the base rule and the higher ceiling as something to confirm for your address rather than assume.
  • The $750,000 wind-borne debris bar. Effective January 1, 2009, a personal lines residential structure in the wind-borne debris region with an insured value of $750,000 or more is not eligible for Citizens unless it has the required opening protections. The Florida Building Code defines that region as areas within hurricane-prone regions lying within one mile of the coastal mean high water line where the ultimate design wind speed is 130 mph or greater, or anywhere it reaches 140 mph or greater. Whether a given Bonita Springs, Estero, or Naples address falls inside it is an address-level determination, not a city-level one, and we won't guess it for you here.

Read together, the picture is clear. At the upper end of our market — and $700,000 is not the upper end of Bonita Springs or Naples — Citizens may not be an option at any price. The private market is the only market, and the private market underwrites.

The wider environment is at least moving the right way. Florida's Office of Insurance Regulation reported in May 2026 that 20 insurers had entered the market since the reforms and that the 180-day average homeowners rate request stood at −2.9%, against +0.7% a year earlier. We have written separately on how thin that relief actually feels here, and that remains our view: a softening market helps your buyer qualify, but it does not make an uninsurable home insurable.

Timing: when the quote actually lands

Buyers rarely price insurance before writing an offer. They price it once under contract, usually alongside the inspection period, often only after the lender asks for a binder. That is why the 34 days matter.

Two things follow. An insurance problem surfaces when your leverage is lowest — your home is off the market and you have made plans around a closing date. And the buyer's first quote is almost always the worst number available, because an underwriter handed no documentation assumes the worst about everything it cannot see.

That second point is the one sellers can change. A four-point inspection and a wind mitigation report in the listing file mean the buyer's carrier is rating a documented home rather than an unknown one. Our guide to Florida's four-point and wind mitigation inspections covers what those reports contain and what the April 2026 form revision changed. And if you carry flood coverage, an NFIP policy can often be assigned to your buyer at closing with its rating history intact — one of the few insurance levers that is purely paperwork.

Where sellers lose the deal

Three patterns, in order of how often we see them.

Treating roof age as a conversation rather than a document. The permit record decides this, not recollection, and s. 627.7011(5)(d) keys the calculation to full-surface replacement. Find out what the record says before a buyer's underwriter does.

Assuming Citizens is the floor. If your dwelling replacement cost is near or above $700,000, it may not be available to your buyer at all. That changes which buyers can realistically close on your home, which is a pricing input, not a footnote.

Letting the buyer's first quote stand as the number. A quote built on missing documentation is not a verdict. It is the opening position of a carrier that was handed nothing.

On re-roofing before listing versus offering a credit, the honest answer is that it turns on your roof's documented remaining life, your price band, and what your buyer's financing will tolerate. That deserves its own analysis rather than a rule of thumb from a blog post.

How we handle the insurance question on a listing

We treat insurability as listing preparation, in the same breath as pricing and presentation, because in this market it moves the closing date more reliably than either.

What would we look at first if you called us before listing?

We would start with the permit record on your roof rather than your memory of the work, because the statute measures age from the last full-surface replacement and that date is frequently earlier than owners expect. Then we would want a dwelling replacement cost figure early, not late — that single number decides whether Citizens is even in the conversation for your buyer, and if it isn't, it narrows the pool of people who can close on your home in a way that belongs in the pricing discussion rather than in a week-three renegotiation.

If your insured value sits near the statutory threshold, we would want to know the state of your opening protection — that is the difference between a hard eligibility bar and an ordinary underwriting question. And we would get the documentation a carrier needs into the listing file before the first showing, so your buyer's quote is built on what your home actually is rather than what an underwriter assumes when handed nothing.

What that buys you is an ordinary 34 days instead of an anxious one. Frequently asked questions

Can a Florida insurance company refuse to cover my home just because the roof is old?

Not solely on roof age if the roof is under 15 years old — s. 627.7011(5)(b), Florida Statutes, says an insurer may not refuse to issue or renew on that basis alone. At 15 years or older, s. 627.7011(5)(c) requires the carrier to allow a roof inspection by an authorized inspector, at your expense, before demanding replacement as a condition of coverage, and it may not refuse solely on roof age if that inspection shows 5 years or more of useful life remaining.

Does Citizens have to insure my home if private carriers turn it down?

No, and the test runs the opposite way from what most sellers expect. Under s. 627.351(6), a primary residence is not eligible for a Citizens policy unless an authorized insurer's premium for comparable coverage is more than 20 percent greater than Citizens' premium for comparable coverage. A private offer that is merely expensive does not open the Citizens door.

My home is worth more than $700,000. Can my buyer still use Citizens?

Generally no. A structure with a dwelling replacement cost of $700,000 or more is not eligible for Citizens coverage. The statute lifts that to under $1 million in counties where the Office of Insurance Regulation finds there is not a reasonable degree of competition, which is worth confirming for your address rather than assuming.

Should I get the four-point and wind mitigation inspections before I list?

In most cases yes, because they change what your buyer's carrier is rating. Without them an underwriter prices the unknown, and the unknown is always expensive. Our four-point and wind mitigation guide covers what each report contains.

The short version: your list price is a decision you make, but your home's insurability is mostly a set of published rules that already apply to it. Finding out which ones is a morning's work before you list, and a crisis after you are under contract.
