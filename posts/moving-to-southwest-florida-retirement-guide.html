Moving to Southwest Florida, FL: the 2026 complete retirement guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·September 26, 2026·10 min read

Is Southwest Florida still a good place to retire in 2026?

Yes, but only if you plan around the real numbers instead of the postcard. Home prices across Bonita Springs, Estero, Naples, Fort Myers, and Cape Coral are flat or down year-over-year, and homes are sitting on the market 66 to 96 days, which hands retirees real negotiating room for the first time in years. At the same time, homeowners insurance premiums have climbed roughly 75% statewide since 2021, and a small but growing number of retirees are relocating again to "halfback" states like the Carolinas. Retirees who do their homework on insurance, HOA financials, and community fit are still finding genuine value here. Retirees who don't are the ones showing up in the headlines.

We hear a version of the same question constantly from retirees exploring a move here: is Florida still worth it, or has it gotten too expensive? It's a fair question, and it deserves a straight answer instead of a sales pitch. This guide walks through what retiring to Southwest Florida actually costs in 2026, which cities and communities make sense for different budgets and lifestyles, and the mistakes that turn excited new retirees into the "halfback" statistics you may have read about.

Why Southwest Florida still wins for retirees in 2026

Florida's core financial advantage for retirees hasn't changed: there is no state income tax on Social Security benefits, pension income, IRA or 401(k) distributions, or capital gains. If you're relocating from New York, New Jersey, Illinois, Michigan, or California, that difference alone can represent thousands to tens of thousands of dollars a year that stays in your pocket instead of going to a state treasury.

Add in year-round warm weather, direct Gulf of Mexico access, and a market that's genuinely more negotiable than it's been in years, and the fundamentals are still strong. But a recent HireAHelper migration study puts a finer point on the current moment: Florida's net gain of retirees (age 65+) nearly flattened in 2025, with about 45,696 moving in and 44,881 moving out, a net gain of just 815 people. Compare that to South Carolina, which added over 41,548 retirement-age residents the same year.

The retirees leaving Florida aren't leaving because the weather changed. They're leaving because the math stopped working for their specific situation — usually insurance, sometimes HOA fees, occasionally a community that didn't fit how they actually wanted to live.

That's not a reason to rule out Southwest Florida. It's a reason to get the numbers right before you commit, which is exactly where local guidance earns its keep.

Choosing your corner of Southwest Florida

"Southwest Florida" isn't one market. Each city and corridor attracts a different kind of retiree, and the right fit depends on budget, whether golf or boating matters more, and how much amenity-rich structure you want.

Naples carries the highest price point in the region, with luxury golf communities, beachfront condos, and the most established retiree infrastructure in Southwest Florida.

Bonita Springs and Estero sit in the middle of the price range with some of the newest master-planned and active-adult communities in the market, including Valencia Bonita, a 55+ community built around a resort-style amenity center.

Fort Myers offers the most affordable entry point among the core cities and is currently a genuine buyer's market, with a Redfin Compete Score of 28 out of 100 and homes selling around 5% below asking. Pelican Preserve is the area's best-known 55+ community, and Colonial Country Club gives retirees a choice between its golf-mandatory Golf Village (roughly $10,335 a year in dues) and its no-golf Lakes Village (roughly $4,880 a year), which is worth understanding before you fall in love with either side.

Cape Coral is the region's boating and canal-lifestyle market, with a large share of homes carrying no HOA at all, lower overhead than most gated communities, and a median sale price around $379,749 to $385,000 that's still up 5.8% year-over-year even as roughly 41% of active listings have taken a price cut.

What retirement actually costs in Southwest Florida in 2026

Housing prices vary sharply by city. Fort Myers sits around $329,835 median, Cape Coral around $379,749 to $385,000, and Bonita Springs around $544,640, which is actually down about 1% year-over-year with homes averaging 90 days on market. Naples and the master-planned Estero corridor typically run higher still, especially for new construction or golf-deeded homes.

Property taxes run roughly 0.79% of assessed value once you file for the homestead exemption, and Florida's Save Our Homes provision caps future assessment increases at 3% a year once you're homesteaded — a meaningful long-term protection for anyone on a fixed retirement income. If you're moving from another Florida homestead, your Save Our Homes benefit may even be portable to your new home.

Insurance is the number that catches retirees off guard. Statewide premiums rose roughly 75% between 2021 and 2025, compared with about 38% nationally. One widely reported case involved a retiree's premium on a paid-off $620,000 coastal home climbing from $4,200 to over $14,200 in five years. Budget $3,000 to $8,000 or more a year for a standard homeowners policy depending on the home's age and construction, and understand that flood insurance is assessed parcel by parcel, not neighborhood-wide — two homes on the same street can sit in different FEMA flood zones, with flood coverage adding another $1,500 to $4,000 or more a year in Zone AE or VE.

HOA and CDD fees range from zero in many Cape Coral neighborhoods and communities like San Carlos Estates to well over $10,000 a year in golf-mandatory communities. Before you fall for a clubhouse, read our guide on how to evaluate a Florida HOA before buying and our breakdown of CDD fees in Bonita Springs and Estero, since CDD debt assessments can run for decades and materially change your monthly cost.

Healthcare and everyday life

Lee County is served by Lee Health, the region's major hospital system, with multiple campuses including Gulf Coast Medical Center and Lee Memorial Hospital, and an announced expansion of its Fort Myers campus to keep pace with the county's growing retirement-age population. Collier County is served by Naples Comprehensive Health (NCH), which operates hospital campuses in and around Naples.

Day-to-day life leans toward golf, pickleball, boating, and the beach, but there's real cultural depth too — Barbara B. Mann Performing Arts Hall and the IMAG History & Science Center in Fort Myers, Fifth Avenue South's shopping and dining in Naples, and spring training baseball each February and March. Snowbird season, roughly January through April, brings noticeably heavier traffic on US-41 and I-75, which is worth experiencing in person before you commit to a specific commute or errand routine.

What out-of-state retirees need to know before closing

Right now, the market favors buyers more than it has in years. That's real leverage, but it comes with Florida-specific mechanics that catch first-time Florida buyers off guard.

Title companies handle closings in Florida, not attorneys, though you can still hire one to review your contract if you want the extra layer of review.

Four-point and wind mitigation inspections are standard for homes over 10 to 15 years old and are separate from your general home inspection. They document your roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC systems, plus hurricane-resistance features, and they can meaningfully lower your insurance quote — but only if you get quotes before you waive any contingencies, not after.

If you're buying into a community, expect an HOA estoppel letter and possibly a board approval process before closing. Sight-unseen purchases happen in this market, but retirees especially benefit from at least one in-person visit — ideally including a summer visit — before signing a contract. The mistakes that turn retirees into "halfbacks"

"Halfback" retirees are people who moved from the Northeast or Midwest to Florida, then relocated again to a state like North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, or Tennessee that sits roughly halfway back toward where they started. It's a real and growing pattern, and it's almost never about the weather. It's almost always one of these mistakes:

  • Only visiting in the winter. January in Naples and July in Naples are two different climates. Experience a Southwest Florida summer, even briefly, before you commit.
  • Getting one insurance quote instead of three. Premiums vary widely by carrier and by specific construction features. A single quote is not a market read.
  • Buying the amenities without reading the HOA's reserve study. A beautiful clubhouse funded by an underfunded reserve fund becomes your special assessment in year three.
  • Ignoring CDD debt-assessment schedules. Some run through the 2040s. Know what you're actually committing to, not just this year's number.
  • Assuming "no income tax" solves the whole equation. It's real savings, but it has to be weighed against your specific insurance and HOA costs, not treated as a blanket offset.

Retirees who avoid these five mistakes are overwhelmingly the ones who stay and thrive here. That's exactly the kind of groundwork we walk every retiring client through before they write an offer, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Southwest Florida still a good place to retire in 2026?

Yes, for retirees who plan around the real costs. Home prices across Bonita Springs, Estero, Naples, Fort Myers, and Cape Coral are flat to down year-over-year, and homes are sitting on the market 66-96 days, which gives retirees genuine negotiating room. The retirees who struggle are usually the ones who didn't shop insurance across multiple carriers, verify HOA and CDD fee schedules, or choose a community that matched their actual lifestyle rather than a marketing brochure.

How much does homeowners insurance really cost for retirees in Southwest Florida?

Budget $3,000 to $8,000 or more per year for a standard homeowners policy, depending on the home's age, construction, and wind mitigation features, plus another $1,500 to $4,000 or more per year if the specific parcel sits in FEMA Flood Zone AE or VE. Statewide premiums rose roughly 75% between 2021 and 2025, nearly double the 38% national increase, so getting quotes from at least three carriers before you write an offer is essential, not optional.

Do retirees pay Florida income tax on Social Security, pensions, or retirement account withdrawals?

No. Florida has no state income tax, so Social Security benefits, pension income, IRA and 401(k) distributions, and capital gains are not taxed at the state level. For retirees relocating from high-tax states like New York, New Jersey, Illinois, Michigan, or California, this can mean thousands to tens of thousands of dollars in annual savings, though it should be weighed against Florida's higher insurance and HOA costs rather than viewed in isolation.

What's the difference between retiring in Naples, Bonita Springs, Estero, Fort Myers, and Cape Coral?

Naples carries the highest price point with luxury golf and beachfront communities. Bonita Springs and Estero offer newer master-planned and active-adult communities in the middle of the price range. Fort Myers has the most affordable entry point among the core cities with a genuine buyer's market. Cape Coral offers canal and boating access with lower HOA overhead and more homes with no HOA at all. The right fit depends on budget, whether golf or boating matters more, and how much amenity-rich structure you want versus independence.

What is "halfback" migration, and how do I avoid becoming one?

"Halfback" retirees are people who moved from the Northeast or Midwest to Florida, then relocated again to states like the Carolinas, Georgia, or Tennessee that sit halfway back toward where they started. Recent data shows Florida's net retiree migration nearly flattened in 2025, largely due to insurance costs and affordability strain. You avoid becoming a statistic by shopping insurance before you buy, verifying a community's HOA and CDD financial health up front, and choosing a home and community that fits your actual budget and lifestyle rather than the one that looked best in January.

The retirees who thrive in Southwest Florida in 2026 are the ones who treat this like the financial decision it is, not just a lifestyle decision. Get real insurance quotes, read the HOA financials, visit in the off-season, and pick the city and community that actually fits how you want to spend your mornings — not just how the brochure photographs.
