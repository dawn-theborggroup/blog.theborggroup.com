How to check for open permits before buying in Estero FL: 2026 buyer's guide

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 15, 2026·9 min read

How do you check for open permits before buying a home in Estero, FL?

Start by working out which office holds the home's permit history: Lee County handled building permits for the Village of Estero until 2016, and the Village's own Community Development Department has handled them since. Pull the permit history from both eras, compare it against the roof, pool, lanai, windows, and additions you can actually see, and have the open-permit and lien search ordered during your inspection period — not in the last days before closing. Florida law protects an arm's-length buyer from being fined over a previous owner's open permit, but it doesn't make unfinished or unpermitted work go away, and a standard owner's title policy generally won't cover it.

An open permit is one of those problems that's cheap to find early and expensive to find late. Found during your inspection period, it's a conversation with the seller. Found after closing, it's your project, your contractor, and sometimes your insurance problem.

It's also one of the questions we hear more often from buyers looking at resale homes in Estero, where so many houses have had a new roof, a pool cage, impact windows, or a generator added since they were built — and where a lot of that work happened in the rebuilding years after Hurricane Ian.

Why open permits matter more than buyers expect

A building permit stays attached to the property, not the person who pulled it. When a roofer, pool company, or homeowner pulls a permit and the job never gets a final inspection, that permit sits open on the address until someone closes it.

There are really three different situations hiding under the phrase "permit problem," and they don't carry the same risk:

  • Open permit — the work was permitted, but the final inspection never happened. The work may be perfectly fine. It just was never signed off.
  • Expired permit — the permit aged out before the job was inspected and finaled. Under Florida law, if the work was substantially complete, it can often be closed without pulling a brand-new permit.
  • Never permitted — the lanai was enclosed, the garage converted, or the windows swapped with no permit at all. This is the hardest one, because there's no record to find. You find it by noticing that the house and the paperwork don't match.

Here's the part most buyers don't know. Thanks to a 2019 open-permits law that took effect October 1 of that year, section 553.79(17) of the Florida Statutes now says a local building department may not deny a permit to, issue a violation to, or fine an arm's-length purchaser solely because a previous owner's permit was never closed. That's real protection. But the same subsection keeps the building department's remedies against the owner and contractor listed on the permit — and nothing in the law certifies that the work itself was done right.

And your title insurance usually isn't the backstop people assume. Florida real estate attorneys have pointed out for years that open permits are generally not covered by an owner's title policy, because they fall under the policy's exclusions for governmental regulations. That's why the search has to happen before you close, not after.

An open permit found during your inspection period is a negotiation. The same permit found after closing is your bill.

A step-by-step guide to checking permits on an Estero home

This is the "what to know and what to watch for" version — the questions that keep you from getting surprised.

  1. Confirm which government holds the record. An Estero mailing address doesn't automatically mean the home is inside the Village. Some addresses with Estero ZIP codes sit in unincorporated Lee County, and their permits live with the county. Our guide on how to check if a home is inside the Village of Estero walks through how to settle that first.
  2. Account for the 2016 split. According to the Village Clerk's office, Lee County Community Development provided building and permitting services for Estero until 2016. Work done before then is in Lee County's records. Work done since is with the Village's Community Development Department (239-221-5036). A home built in 2008 with a 2021 re-roof can have its history in two places.
  3. Pull the history for the right address. For a condo or coach home, permits for roofs and structural work are often filed under the building's base address, not the unit. A unit-only search can come back clean when it isn't.
  4. Hold the permit list up against the house. The Village's own "Do I Need a Permit" brochure lists pools and spas, additions, garage doors, and generators among the work that requires a permit. If the home has a newer roof, an enclosed lanai, impact windows, a generator, or a pool cage and there's no matching permit, that's your question for the seller.
  5. Sort what you find into open, expired, or never permitted. Each one gets solved differently, costs differently, and belongs in a different conversation with the seller.
  6. Get the open-permit and lien search ordered early. In the Village, lien search and open-permit requests go through a designated form with a $50 processing fee. The search should come back while you still have inspection-period leverage, not a week before closing.
  7. Read what the search doesn't promise. The Village notes that it does not certify that unpermitted work was never performed. A clean open-permit report is good news — it isn't proof that every improvement was permitted.

The Florida-specific wrinkles

The new Village portal requires an account. On September 9, 2024, the Village of Estero switched to a new permitting system, and the public portal requires registering for a login. Older Lee County records are requested through the county's public records custodian.

Old permits can sometimes be closed administratively. Under section 553.79(16)(c), a building department may close a permit six years after it was issued, even without a final inspection, if it finds no apparent safety hazards. That's a tool for the building official — not something a buyer can count on.

A property owner can close a permit. Section 553.79(16)(a) lets the owner close a permit using the original contractor, another licensed contractor, or as an owner-builder. That's usually the seller's job before closing, and it's worth getting in writing who handles it.

Your insurance cares about permit dates. Under section 627.7011(5), a Florida insurer can't refuse to write or renew a policy solely because of the age of a roof under 15 years old. Roof age is commonly documented through the roof permit, so a missing or open re-roof permit can turn into an insurance question, too. That's one more reason we line up permits with your four-point and wind mitigation inspections.

The municipal estoppel isn't a shortcut. The Village of Estero stopped processing estoppel requests on April 22, 2025, and it routes lien and open-permit questions through its records request process. Don't assume one certificate covers everything.

Red flags worth slowing down for

  • A roof, pool cage, or lanai that's clearly newer than the house, with no permit on file in either era.
  • Repair work after Hurricane Ian with no permit history. FEMA raised concerns in 2024 about unpermitted post-Ian repairs in Lee County and several of its municipalities, Estero included.
  • A seller who says the permit "is basically closed" or "just needs a final." That may be true — and it should be finished before closing, not promised.
  • A garage conversion, enclosed lanai, or added room that changes the square footage shown on the property appraiser's record.
  • An "as is" listing with a long permit history and no final inspections. As-is changes who pays to fix things, not whether the problem exists.
  • A Seller's Property Disclosure that's silent on improvements you can plainly see. Our post on the Florida Seller's Property Disclosure covers what that form is meant to tell you.

How a buyer's agent protects you here

Most of this isn't hard once you know where to look. The trouble is timing — permit issues surface late when nobody's watching for them, and late is when they kill deals. It's one of the quieter reasons deals fall through in Southwest Florida.

What would we look at first if you called us about a home in Estero?

The first thing we'd want to know is the home's story, not just its address. When was it built, what's been replaced, and when? A 2006 home with a 2023 roof and a 2019 pool cage tells us exactly which era of records we need, and which counter holds each one.

Then we'd walk the house with the permit picture in our heads. The things we flag are the ones that change your money: a roof whose age your insurer will want documented, an enclosed lanai that might be counted as living space, impact windows that matter for your wind mitigation credits, or a generator with no final inspection.

If something's open or missing, we want it on the table while you still have your inspection period, so the fix becomes the seller's responsibility to resolve or price in — not a surprise in your first year. We've been doing this in Southwest Florida since The Borg Group was established in 2005, and the pattern doesn't change: permit questions are easy to handle early and miserable to handle late.

What you get when it's handled well is simple. By the time you decide, the house, the paperwork, and the insurance quote all describe the same property. That's the part we take care of for you.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be fined for an open permit the previous owner pulled in Florida?

Generally, no. Section 553.79(17) of the Florida Statutes says a local building department may not deny a permit to, cite, or fine an arm's-length buyer solely because a previous owner's permit wasn't closed. The work itself still may need to be finished and inspected, and the department keeps its remedies against the owner and contractor on the permit.

Where are permit records for a home in the Village of Estero?

It depends on when the work was done. Lee County provided building and permitting services for Estero until 2016, so older records are requested from Lee County. Records from 2016 on are with the Village of Estero's Community Development Department at 239-221-5036.

Does title insurance cover open permits in Florida?

Usually not. Open permits are generally treated as governmental-regulation matters that an owner's title policy excludes, which is why the open-permit and lien search needs to come back before closing.

How much does an open-permit search cost in Estero?

The Village of Estero charges a $50 processing fee for lien search and open-permit requests submitted through its designated form. Your title company or closing agent typically orders the municipal search as part of the transaction.

What's the difference between an open permit and unpermitted work?

An open permit means the work was permitted but never received a final inspection. Unpermitted work was never permitted at all, so it won't show up in a permit search — you find it by comparing the home's improvements against its permit history.

The bottom line

In Estero, a home's permit history can live in two places, and the search only tells you about permits that exist. Check the right counter for each era, match the paperwork to the house, and get the open-permit search back while you still have room to negotiate.
