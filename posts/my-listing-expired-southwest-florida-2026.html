My listing expired in Southwest Florida. What happens now? 2026

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 28, 2026·8 min read

What happens when your listing expires in Southwest Florida?

Your home comes off the market, your listing agreement ends, and the decision about what happens next is yours again. The MLS status changes to expired, the home stops showing up as active to buyers, and you’ll likely start hearing from a lot of agents within hours. Your agreement may still include a protection period that matters if you sell to a buyer who saw the home while it was listed. An expiration isn’t a verdict on your home. It’s the moment to find out what didn’t work before you try again.

“My listing expired — now what?” is a question we hear more often every fall, and late December is when it peaks. A lot of Southwest Florida listings are written to end on December 31, right before seasonal buyers return in January. If yours is one of them, the next few weeks matter more than they look.

Here’s what actually changes on the day your listing expires, what the market looks like right now, and why the first week after expiration is for understanding, not reacting.

What the Southwest Florida market looks like right now

These are the newest numbers we could open today. They explain why so many homes are reaching their expiration date.

  • Lee County inventory: Realtor.com’s October 2026 data, published through the St. Louis Fed’s FRED database, counted 9,635 active listings in Lee County, down from 11,042 a year earlier. Of those, 2,684 had taken a price cut — about 28%.
  • Collier County inventory: the same source counted 5,020 active listings in Collier in October (5,635 a year earlier), with 1,004 price reductions — about 20%.
  • Time on market: median days on market was 80 in Lee and 84 in Collier in October, both shorter than a year ago (91 and 93). Homes that are priced and presented right are still selling.
  • Closed sales: Florida Realtors reported 1,115 single-family sales in the Cape Coral–Fort Myers metro (Lee County) in October 2026, at a $389,900 median, flat from a year ago. The Naples metro (Collier County) had 362 single-family sales at a $780,000 median, up 4.9%.
  • Condos: Collier condo and townhouse sales fell 25.9% year over year in October, to 260, at a $415,750 median. Lee condo sales rose 12.7%, to 373, at a $269,000 median.

Our own Southwest Florida MLS market ticker (updated November 27, 2026) shows how different each area is right now. Months of supply below is our arithmetic — active listings divided by the monthly sales pace over the last 45 days.

  • Estero single-family: 290 active, 88 pending, 87 sold in 45 days, $725,000 median sale price, 96.7% of list price — about 5 months of supply.
  • Bonita Springs single-family: 276 active, 80 pending, 79 sold, $725,000 median, 96.8% of list — about 5 months.
  • Naples single-family: 2,104 active, 384 pending, 439 sold, $880,000 median, 96.2% of list — about 7 months.
  • Fort Myers single-family: 1,226 active, 224 pending, 310 sold, $461,000 median — about 6 months.
  • Cape Coral single-family: 2,454 active, 258 pending, 333 sold, $380,000 median — about 11 months.
  • Condos: roughly 7 months of supply in Estero, 8 in Bonita Springs, and 9 in Naples.

What that means for you: buyers have choices, and they’re comparing. Homes are still selling in every one of these areas, at roughly 96% to 98% of list price. The listings that expire are usually the ones that didn’t line up with what buyers were comparing them against — on price, on condition, or on how they were presented.

What changes the day your listing expires

Your home disappears from the active market

Once the agreement ends, the listing comes off the MLS as an active property, and the major portals that pull from the MLS stop showing it as for sale. Showings stop. If a buyer was circling, they now have to find you another way. Your agent relationship under that agreement is over unless you sign a new one.

Your phone starts ringing

Expected and very common: calls, texts, letters, and door knocks from agents and investors, sometimes dozens in the first day. That’s because an expired listing is one of the few times agents are allowed to approach a seller directly. Under the Realtor Code of Ethics, agents can’t solicit you while you’re exclusively listed with another brokerage. Once the agreement has ended, they can. Ethical agents will ask first whether you’ve already relisted, and phone calls are still subject to Do Not Call rules.

You don’t owe anyone a decision that week. The volume is a sign that agents know homes like yours sell — not a sign that you have to pick someone by Friday.

A protection period may still apply

Florida listing agreements commonly include a protection period — a set number of days after the agreement ends. In the standard Florida Realtors exclusive right of sale form, the number of days is filled in when the agreement is signed. If you sell or agree to sell during that window to a buyer who learned about the home while it was listed, the original broker may still be owed a fee. That same standard form says no fee is due if the home is relisted and sold through another broker.

Not every agreement uses that form or that wording, so the only answer that counts is what your agreement says. Read it, ask your former listing agent or their broker if anything is unclear, and talk to a real estate attorney if real money is at stake. This is general information, not legal advice.

Timing: why a December 31 expiration is a turning point

Nationally, the first week of the year has historically been the biggest week for expired, canceled, and withdrawn listings. Inman, citing REDX data, reported about 129,686 of them in the first week of 2024 alone. Southwest Florida adds its own twist: our busiest buying season starts just as many of those listings end.

Seasonal buyers return after the holidays, and January through April is when many of them tour and write offers. That means a home that expires on December 31 has a short runway. Relaunch the same listing too fast and it meets January buyers looking exactly like the home they ignored in the fall. Wait too long and you miss the strongest weeks of season, while the costs of owning keep adding up. (We broke those costs down in the real cost of carrying a Southwest Florida home that isn’t selling.)

The sweet spot is a short, deliberate pause — long enough to understand what happened and change the right thing, short enough to be back on the market while season is in full swing. If you’re weighing a pause before your expiration date, our take on pulling a home off the market for the holidays and our seasonal guide to selling in Southwest Florida cover the calendar side.

Common mistakes after a listing expires

  • Hiring the loudest caller. The agent who calls first or promises the highest number hasn’t necessarily looked at why your home didn’t sell. A higher price promise can put you right back where you started.
  • Relisting the same home at the same price. A new listing date doesn’t change what buyers see. If price, condition, and presentation are unchanged, the result usually is too — and the listing history follows the home.
  • Blaming the market for everything. Homes are selling in every area above. If similar homes nearby went under contract and yours didn’t, the market isn’t the whole story.
  • Assuming it was all price. Sometimes it was. Sometimes it was the roof and the insurance quote, the photos, or showing access. Cutting price when the issue was condition gives away money without fixing the problem.
  • Ignoring the protection period. Selling privately to someone who toured the home during the listing, without checking your agreement, can create an expensive surprise.
  • Waiting until spring to decide. In Southwest Florida, “we’ll try again later” can mean missing the January-to-April window entirely.

How The Borg Group markets listings that need a second launch

A relaunch only works if something real has changed — and if buyers can see it. When we take on a home that has been on the market before, we treat it like a new listing with history, not a repeat. That means a fresh look at pricing against what’s actually selling in your area today, presentation that gives the home a new first impression, and a plan built around the January buyers who are about to arrive.

What would we look at first if you called us about a listing that just expired?

We’d start with the record. The MLS shows how your home was priced, how long it sat, and how many buyers saw it — and it shows what those buyers ended up buying instead. That comparison usually tells us more than any feedback form, because it shows what your home was up against. (If you had plenty of showings and no offers, here’s why that pattern is its own signal.)

Then we’d look at the home the way a Southwest Florida buyer’s insurer and inspector will: roof, insurability, flood zone, and anything that could stall a deal after an offer. In this market, those questions sink more sales than most sellers realize.

From there, the answer is usually specific. It’s rarely “cut the price and hope.” It’s the right change, made once, timed for season. When that’s handled well, your relaunch doesn’t look like the same listing coming back — it looks like a home buyers haven’t seen yet. That’s the part we take care of for you.

The bottom line

An expired listing is a pause, not a failure. The market is still moving, buyers are about to return, and the homes that sell the second time around are the ones where someone found out what went wrong and changed it. Take a breath, read your agreement, and don’t let the flood of calls make the decision for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens when my listing expires in Florida?

Your listing agreement ends, the home comes off the MLS as an active listing, and showings stop. You are free to relist with the same agent, sign with a different one, or wait. If your agreement has a protection period, it may still matter for buyers who saw the home while it was listed.

Why are so many agents calling me after my listing expired?

An expired listing is one of the few times agents may contact a seller directly. Under the Realtor Code of Ethics, agents cannot solicit you while you are exclusively listed with another brokerage, but once the agreement ends they can. You do not have to make a decision right away.

What is a protection period in a Florida listing agreement?

It is a set number of days after the listing ends. If you sell during that window to a buyer who learned about the home while it was listed, the original broker may still be owed a fee. In the standard Florida Realtors exclusive right of sale form, no fee is due if the home is relisted and sold through another broker, but agreements vary, so read yours and ask a real estate attorney if you are unsure.

Should I relist right away after my listing expires?

Usually not the same day, and usually not unchanged. Relisting the same home at the same price tends to repeat the same result. In Southwest Florida, a short pause to find out what held buyers back, timed so you are back on the market as seasonal buyers return in January, is often the better move.

Can I relist with the same agent after my listing expires?

Yes. Many sellers do, with a new agreement and a changed plan. What matters is that price, condition, or presentation actually changes, and that whoever you work with can explain what held buyers back the first time.
