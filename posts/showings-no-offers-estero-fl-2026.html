Lots of showings, no offers in Estero FL? What buyers are telling you 2026

By Mark & Dawn Borg·November 27, 2026·8 min read

Why are buyers touring my Estero home but not making offers?

Because something between the photos and the purchase isn’t holding up. Steady showings tell you the price and the listing are good enough to get buyers in the door. No offers tell you that what buyers see in person, learn afterward, or compare it against is changing their mind. In Estero right now, that usually comes down to one of three things: the condition doesn’t match the price, the insurance or roof story scares them off after the showing, or a similar home down the road looked like a better deal.

“We’ve had all these showings — why hasn’t anyone made an offer?” is one of the most frustrating places a seller can be. It’s also one of the most common seller questions we see online right now, from Reddit threads to national surveys of agents.

The good news is that a home with showings and no offers is closer to selling than a home with no showings at all. Buyers are already interested. The job now is finding out where they’re dropping off.

What the Estero market looks like right now

Here are the newest numbers we could open today.

  • Estero single-family: our Southwest Florida MLS market ticker (updated November 26, 2026) shows 292 active listings, 86 pending, and 87 sales in the last 45 days. The median sale price is $725,000, and homes are selling at 96.7% of list price. By our arithmetic, that’s about 5 months of supply.
  • Estero condos: 273 active, 52 pending, 59 sold in 45 days, a $338,900 median, and 96.6% of list — closer to 7 months of supply by the same math.
  • Lee County overall: Realtor.com’s October 2026 inventory data (published through the St. Louis Fed’s FRED database) counted 9,635 active listings in Lee County, and 2,684 of them had taken a price cut. That’s more than one in four.
  • Time on market: the same data shows a median of 80 days on market in Lee County in October, down from 91 a year earlier.
  • Closed sales: Florida Realtors reported 1,115 single-family sales in Lee County in October 2026, at a $389,900 median, flat from a year ago.
  • Rates: Freddie Mac’s 30-year fixed average was 7.21% on November 25, 2026, down from 7.28% the week before.

Two things matter here. Estero buyers are active — 86 single-family homes are under contract heading into the holidays. And buyers have choices. With roughly five months of supply and more than a quarter of Lee County listings already reduced, a buyer who tours your home has probably toured three or four others that week too.

What sellers can expect: where buyers drop off

Think of every showing as a three-step test. Your home passed the first step when the buyer booked the appointment. The question is which of the next three steps is stopping them.

1. During the showing: the home doesn’t match the photos

Your listing promised something, and the walk-through has to deliver it. When it doesn’t, buyers rarely say so directly. They just move on.

The usual culprits are things the camera doesn’t catch:

  • Smell — pets, smoke, or that closed-up, musty Florida smell from a home that’s been sitting with the air conditioning turned up
  • Visible maintenance — water stains, a tired lanai screen, a pool that needs work, or obvious DIY fixes
  • Darker or smaller rooms than the wide-angle photos suggested
  • Dated finishes at a price that assumes updates

In a national HomeLight survey of 715 top agents (fielded August–September 2026), 47% said price was the most likely reason a home gets showings but no offers, and 19% pointed to condition. Those two are usually linked. Buyers aren’t saying the home is bad. They’re saying it’s not worth this price in this condition.

Strong photos are still essential — we covered why in why professional photos and video matter when selling. But when showings are steady and offers aren’t, the photos are doing their job. The gap is in the room.

2. After the showing: the insurance quote or the roof

This is the Southwest Florida step that sellers from other states often don’t see coming. A buyer can love your home on Saturday and call an insurance agent on Monday. If the quote comes back high — or the agent asks about roof age, the electrical panel, or the flood zone — that buyer can disappear without a word.

Buyers in Estero commonly ask about:

  • Roof age and type, since it drives whether a home can be insured and at what cost
  • A recent wind mitigation report, which can lower the premium when the home has qualifying features
  • A four-point inspection (roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC), which many insurers require on older homes
  • The flood zone and whether there’s an elevation certificate
  • HOA or CDD fees on top of the mortgage

If your home has a newer roof or strong wind mitigation features, buyers need to know before they call an insurer — not after. We go deeper on this in can your buyer actually insure your home?

3. In comparison: a better deal down the road

Sometimes nothing is wrong with your home. Another one simply looked like more for the money. In Estero, that competition often includes new construction, where builders may be offering rate buydowns or closing-cost help that a resale seller isn’t.

The best evidence here isn’t the feedback form. It’s what the buyers who toured your home bought instead. If they went under contract on a home with a similar price and an updated kitchen, or a newer roof, or a lower HOA fee, that tells you exactly what the market valued.

Reading buyer feedback: what it says and what it doesn’t

Showing feedback helps, but it’s filtered. Buyers’ agents work for their buyers, and the National Association of Realtors notes that many keep feedback brief and general to protect their client’s negotiating position — especially if the buyer might still make an offer.

So “they loved it, just not the right fit” may be polite code. Here’s how to read the patterns:

  • The same comment from several showings (“smaller than expected,” “needs updating”) is a real signal, even if each one sounds mild.
  • “Price” with no detail usually means the home lost a side-by-side comparison.
  • Silence after a showing often means the buyer moved on quickly.
  • Second showings with no offer can point to something buyers learned after the first visit — insurance, HOA documents, or an inspection worry.

Timing considerations heading into season

Timing works in your favor right now if you act on what buyers are telling you. Many seasonal buyers arrive after the holidays, and January through April is when the most buyers are touring in person. A change made in early December reaches the serious buyers already here. The same change made in mid-January lands alongside a wave of fresh listings.

If you’re thinking about a short break instead, we walked through that decision in should you take your home off the market for the holidays?

Common seller mistakes when showings don’t turn into offers

  • Waiting for “the right buyer.” Fifteen showings with no offer usually means fifteen right buyers said no to something specific.
  • Cutting the price in small steps. Several small reductions can signal pressure without ever reaching the buyers you lost. Our post on days on market versus price reductions explains why.
  • Fixing the wrong thing. New paint won’t solve an insurance problem, and a price cut won’t fix a smell.
  • Keeping roof and insurance details to yourself. If you have a newer roof, impact windows, or a recent wind mitigation report, buyers should see that up front.
  • Ignoring the homes that sold. Active listings show what sellers hope for. Pending and closed sales show what buyers actually paid. See our 2026 pricing strategy guide for how to read the gap.

What would we look at first if you called us about an Estero home with showings but no offers?

First, we’d figure out which step buyers are dropping off at — during the showing, after it, or in comparison. That one question narrows the problem faster than anything else.

Next, we’d look at the feedback as a pattern, not as individual comments, and match it against the homes those buyers went under contract on instead. If three buyers chose homes with newer roofs at similar prices, that’s not a coincidence.

Then we’d check what a buyer learns in the 48 hours after a showing — the likely insurance picture, the flood zone, HOA or CDD costs, and anything an inspector is sure to flag. In Estero, that’s often where a good showing turns into a quiet no.

From there, the fix is usually specific rather than dramatic: getting the right documents in front of buyers early, addressing one or two condition issues, or repositioning the price where it wins the comparison. When it’s handled well, the next round of showings feels different, because buyers aren’t leaving with unanswered questions. That’s the part we take care of for you.

The bottom line

Lots of showings and no offers isn’t bad luck. It’s buyers telling you something specific. Find the step where they’re dropping off, and you’ll know whether the answer is condition, insurance, or price.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I have showings but no offers on my house?

Usually because something buyers see in person, learn after the showing, or compare your home against is changing their minds. In a national HomeLight survey of 715 top agents in 2026, 47% named price as the most likely cause and 19% named condition. In Southwest Florida, insurance and roof questions after the showing are a common third reason.

How many showings should it take to get an offer?

There is no fixed number, but if a steady stream of showings has produced no offers, the pattern itself is the signal. Look at what the buyers who toured your home bought instead. That comparison usually points to the real issue faster than waiting for more showings.

Is buyer feedback after a showing reliable?

It helps, but it is often filtered. Buyers’ agents work for their buyers and may keep feedback brief to protect their client’s negotiating position. Repeated comments across several showings are more reliable than any single one.

Can insurance keep my Florida home from selling?

It can slow it down. Buyers often get an insurance quote after a showing, and roof age, the four-point inspection, wind mitigation features, and the flood zone all affect that quote. Having a recent wind mitigation report and roof details ready can keep an interested buyer moving.

Should I lower my price if I’m getting showings but no offers?

Not automatically. If buyers are walking away over a smell, a repair, or an insurance concern, a price cut may not fix it. Find out which step buyers are dropping off at first, then decide whether price, condition, or documentation is the right change.
