Seller Tips

How to handle multiple offers on your Southwest Florida home without leaving money on the table

By Mark & Dawn Borg·June 17, 2026·8 min read

Receiving multiple offers on your home can feel exciting, but it can also be one of the most important decision points in the selling process.

If you are selling a home in Naples, Bonita Springs, Estero, Fort Myers, Cape Coral, or another Southwest Florida community, the highest offer is not always the strongest offer. The best offer is the one that gives you the strongest combination of price, terms, buyer strength, clean contingencies, closing certainty, and net proceeds.

When multiple buyers are interested in your home at the same time, your goal is not simply to choose the biggest number. Your goal is to choose the offer most likely to close successfully while protecting your bottom line.

Why the highest offer is not always the best offer

It is natural to feel drawn to the highest purchase price. But in real estate, the number at the top of the contract is only one part of the story. A slightly lower offer with cash, strong proof of funds, a shorter inspection period, no appraisal risk, and a flexible closing date may be better than a higher offer with weak financing, a long inspection period, multiple contingencies, or a buyer who still needs to sell another home.

Southwest Florida attracts many different types of buyers, including seasonal residents, retirees, investors, relocating families, remote workers, and second-home buyers. Each buyer may bring a different level of financial strength and certainty. When reviewing multiple offers, sellers should consider: purchase price, financing type, cash versus financed terms, earnest money deposit, inspection period, appraisal contingency, buyer credits or concessions, closing date, buyer's ability to close, contingencies, requested personal property, leaseback or occupancy needs, and seller net proceeds.

The strongest offer is not always the highest offer. It is the offer that gives you the best overall combination of price, terms, certainty, timeline, and net proceeds.

Financing type matters

A cash offer can be attractive because it removes lender approval, appraisal concerns, and certain risk — but that doesn't always mean a cash offer is automatically the best offer. A conventional loan may also be strong, especially when the buyer has a solid pre-approval, strong down payment, and responsive lender. FHA and VA loans can absolutely close successfully, but they may have different appraisal, condition, or loan requirements. The key is understanding how each financing structure affects your risk, timeline, and final net.

Appraisal risk is real

If the sale price ends up higher than the appraised value, the buyer may need to bring additional cash to closing, renegotiate the price, or cancel if the contract allows. This is especially important when multiple offers push a purchase price above recent comparable sales. Sellers should consider: does the buyer have an appraisal contingency, is the buyer willing to cover an appraisal gap, how much cash does the buyer have available, and does the offer price appear realistic based on comparable sales? A higher financed offer may not be stronger if the buyer cannot cover an appraisal shortfall.

Seller credits and concessions reduce your net

A buyer may offer a strong price but ask for closing cost credits, repair credits, rate buydown assistance, or other concessions. Those credits reduce your net proceeds. For example, an offer of $710,000 with a $15,000 seller credit may net less than an offer of $700,000 with no seller credit. When comparing offers, sellers should always look at the estimated net, not just the gross purchase price.

Closing timeline can be just as important as price

A seller who needs time to move, purchase another home, wait for new construction, or coordinate a leaseback may prefer a buyer with a flexible closing timeline. A seller who has already moved may prefer a faster closing to reduce carrying costs such as mortgage payments, HOA fees, insurance, utilities, and property taxes. A strong offer should support both your financial goals and your moving timeline.

How to use a highest and best strategy

When multiple offers arrive, your agent may recommend asking buyers to submit their highest and best offer by a specific deadline. A good highest and best strategy should include a clear submission deadline, consistent communication to all buyer agents, guidance on what terms matter to the seller, a request for complete offer packages, proof of funds or lender documentation, and final terms submitted in writing.

When to reject an offer

Not every offer deserves a counter. You may choose to reject an offer if it is far below market value, includes too many contingencies, has weak financing, asks for excessive concessions, or does not align with your timeline. In a multiple-offer situation, a seller does not have to work with every buyer — the goal is to choose the offer that best supports your sale.

Backup offers can protect you

Even after accepting an offer, it may be wise to keep a backup offer in place. In Florida, buyers may have inspection rights and other contingencies that could allow them to cancel within certain timelines. This can be especially useful if the primary buyer has a longer inspection period, financing concerns, or other risks.

Common mistakes sellers should avoid

Choosing the highest price without reviewing the terms; ignoring buyer financing strength; overlooking inspection periods; forgetting appraisal risk; not calculating seller net proceeds; accepting large seller credits without understanding the net; failing to compare closing timelines; rejecting backup offers too quickly; letting emotions drive the decision; signing before reviewing every detail. The best decision is made with strategy, not pressure.

Fair housing and offer evaluation

Sellers should evaluate offers based on financial and contractual terms, not on personal characteristics of the buyers. Offer decisions should never be based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, disability, familial status, or any other protected class. A professional listing agent will help keep the process focused on objective terms and fair housing compliance.
