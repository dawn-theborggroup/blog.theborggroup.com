Homeowner's insurance in Bonita Springs: what buyers and sellers need to know in 2026

By Mark & Dawn Borg·April 30, 2026·8 min read

Homeowner's insurance in Bonita Springs typically costs $3,000–$10,000 per year depending on the home's age, roof condition, construction type, and proximity to the coast. Since 2020, rates across Southwest Florida have roughly tripled, and many national carriers have exited the state entirely, leaving fewer options and higher prices. For buyers, insurance costs now affect affordability and can derail a deal if not verified early. For sellers, a home's insurability — especially roof age — directly affects your buyer pool and how competitive your listing is.

This is a conversation we have with almost every client now that didn't happen five years ago — not about the market or the mortgage rate, but about insurance.

What happened to Florida's insurance market

Between 2020 and 2024, more than a dozen carriers either left Florida entirely or stopped writing new policies in coastal counties including Lee. Hurricane Ian's September 2022 landfall — direct impact just north of Bonita Springs in Fort Myers — accelerated claims pressure and drove additional carriers to reassess their exposure across Southwest Florida. The result: fewer choices, higher prices, and more scrutiny on what carriers will cover. Rates for comparable policies in Lee County have roughly tripled from 2019 levels for many homeowners, and this isn't settling down quickly — treat insurance as a core part of the transaction, not a week-before-closing afterthought.

For buyers: what to check before you commit

The single most important move: get an insurance quote before your inspection period ends, not after.

Roof age is the defining factor

Most Florida carriers won't write a new policy on a roof over 15–20 years old; some have tightened to 15. A few will insure an older roof but require a wind mitigation inspection first at a higher premium. If you're looking at an early-2000s-or-older Bonita Springs home with an original roof, find out its age before you fall in love — it directly affects coverage, cost, and loan approval.

Request a four-point inspection early

For homes 20–30+ years old, most insurers require a four-point inspection (roofing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC) before binding coverage. Issues — an older panel, polybutylene pipe, aging AC — can lead to denial or exclusions. Order it in the first week of your inspection period, not the last, so you have time to negotiate or walk away if needed.

Get a wind mitigation inspection — it pays for itself

This evaluates roof shape, covering material, deck attachment, roof-to-wall connections, and opening protection. A favorable report — especially on a newer home with a hip roof and impact windows — can reduce your wind premium by 25–45% or more. It runs $75–$150 and typically pays for itself within the first month of coverage. See our full guide to both inspections for the complete breakdown.

Ask for the seller's current policy and CLUE report

Florida's Seller's Property Disclosure requires disclosing known material defects, including prior insurance claims. A CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) report shows claims history — a record of water damage, mold, or wind claims can affect your ability to get coverage or raise your premium even after proper repairs.

We've seen deals fall apart at the finish line because a buyer's insurance quote came back at $14,000 a year on a home they'd budgeted $4,000 for — enough to blow a debt-to-income ratio and kill loan approval entirely.

Buyer's insurance checklist

  • Get a preliminary quote in week one of your inspection period — not week three
  • Check roof age and material before making an offer
  • Order a four-point inspection for any home 20+ years old
  • Request a wind mitigation inspection
  • Ask for the CLUE report and seller's current policy details
  • Verify flood zone and get a separate flood insurance quote if applicable

For sellers: how insurance affects your listing

A home's insurability directly affects how many buyers can close on it and what they're willing to pay. If your roof is over 15 years old, many buyers' lenders will require replacement before or at closing — some buyers will walk rather than negotiate it. A proactive roof replacement, or a credit toward one, is often money well spent given its effect on buyer pool and time on market.

If you have impact windows, a newer roof, and solid roof-to-wall connections, a pre-listing wind mitigation report is a genuine marketing asset — it shows buyers exactly what their insurance savings will look like, real and comparable across competing homes.

Disclose what you know. Florida's Seller's Property Disclosure requires disclosing material facts you're aware of, including prior water intrusion, roof leaks, mold remediation, and insurance claims — when in doubt, disclose. A claim that surfaces in a CLUE report after closing can become a legal issue.

Citizens Insurance: the backup option

Florida's state-run insurer is available to homeowners who can't find private coverage at a comparable rate — many Lee County homeowners have ended up with Citizens, by choice or because private options dried up.

  • Coverage cap: Citizens currently caps residential dwelling coverage at $700,000 — luxury properties above that need supplemental coverage
  • Depopulation program: Citizens actively moves policies to private carriers; your coverage generally stays intact but your premium can change
  • Not always cheapest: Citizens rates have been increasing under state authorization; private options are sometimes now competitive for newer, well-built homes
  • Surplus lines carriers are worth quoting for hard-to-insure properties — an independent agent who knows the Florida market is valuable here

One more thing: flood insurance is separate

This catches a lot of buyers off guard. Homeowner's insurance does not cover flood damage — period. Properties in FEMA AE or VE zones are required by lenders to carry separate flood insurance; even outside mapped zones, private flood coverage is increasingly worth considering.
